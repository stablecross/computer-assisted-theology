# Four-Stage Evaluation Criteria

*Version 13.*

Evaluate the argument in four stages, after completing the pre-analysis map. Report the stages separately, and end with the standing of each thesis. A later stage may send a finding back to an earlier one in one case only: a Stage 3 text may show that a Stage 2 reading of a cited passage was too thin. When that happens, say so in both stages and revise the Stage 2 result there. Stage 4 consequences may never be used as evidence at Stages 1 through 3.

**The same rules bind author and reviewer.** Whoever asserts a proposition must support it. Whoever challenges an inference must identify and demonstrate its defect. Neither party must prove the opposite conclusion merely to show that the other's argument falls short. A reviewer's findings are propositions too, and they are tested by these rules when the review is itself reviewed.

---

## Pre-analysis: map the argument

This is not a stage. It is the setup all four stages depend on, and skipping it is how an evaluator ends up attacking something the thesis does not rest on.

Before beginning, identify:

1. The exact thesis, stated in the document's own words. Where the document states its thesis more than once — a claim sentence, a restatement, a conclusion — quote every statement and note any divergence in width between them. Evaluate against the widest statement. Then report separately the narrowest statement the argument actually supports.
2. Each conclusion the document separately claims.
3. The premises necessary for each conclusion. Where a conclusion has no stated premises, record it as **asserted** rather than argued, and record what warrant is offered for it: evidence cited, an appeal to agreed background, or nothing. An assertion is not a defective argument, and calling it a bad argument is a category mistake rather than a finding — but it is not thereby warranted, and the warrant is tested below. This applies claim by claim: a document that argues well overall can still assert particular conclusions, and those are where defects hide unmarked.
4. Independent or alternative routes to the same conclusion.
5. Supporting observations the document marks as non-load-bearing.
6. Questions the document explicitly brackets.
7. Readings the document explicitly concedes for the sake of argument.
8. The evidentiary corpus both parties have agreed is admissible. Record it before anything else is assessed. The question is not which corpus is the right one — that is not this protocol's business — but which body of evidence the document and its critic have both accepted for the purposes of this argument. Where they have not agreed, say so. A binding review stops there: a disagreement about what counts as evidence is prior to any disagreement about what the evidence says, and running the four stages on top of an unresolved one produces a verdict neither party is bound by. A conditional review may still be run, and it must be labeled as one: *given this corpus, the argument establishes X.* It binds no one who has not accepted the corpus. Record also any method assumption the review relies on that the parties have not agreed, such as whether a later text may govern the reading of an earlier one. Many apparent interpretive disputes are authority disputes conducted as interpretive ones, and naming the corpus is what makes the difference visible.

**A defect in one route defeats the conclusion only if no independent route survives.** Say which route an objection touches before assessing its force.

**But independence is claimed by the document and verified by you, not granted.** Routes may share premises and still be distinct. What matters is which premises each route needs. Map the premises the routes share and the premises each needs alone. A route survives an objection only if it is still sufficient without the premise or inference that objection defeats. A defect in a shared premise reaches every route that needs it. A defect confined to one route does not reach another. So for every route the document presents as independent, say what it shares with the others, and say what it establishes on its own. A document can otherwise multiply weak corroborative routes and treat each defeat as survivable, and an evaluator who accepts the independence claims at face value will report a conclusion as standing when what survives is narrower than what was claimed. When a route falls, state the narrowest conclusion the remaining routes support.

**Scope is checked in both directions.** Do not require the argument to establish more than it claims. Do not let it claim more than it establishes. Both are scope errors, and the second is the easier one to miss, because every individual inference can be sound while the summary sentence quantifies over a wider class than any inference tested. Where the two diverge, say so here, and say which class of case the proof reaches and which it does not.

**When the document is wholly or largely assertive, say so here and change what you run.** Some documents state conclusions without deriving them — confessions, creeds, position statements, executive summaries, verdicts. Only Stage 1 needs an inference, because validity is the only thing that needs one, and running it on an assertion produces a verdict of failure where the correct result is that it does not apply. Stages 2, 3 and 4 all run normally. Stage 2 is the warrant test and is the important one here: an assertion that cites evidence is making the claim Stage 2 exists to check, which is that the cited evidence establishes the stated proposition. Stages 3 and 4 need no premises either, since an asserted conclusion can be stress-tested against the wider evidence and traced for what follows from it.

**An assertion is not exempt from warrant, and the three cases are tested differently.**

- **Asserted with evidence cited.** Runs at Stage 2 exactly as a premise does. The absence of a derivation changes nothing about whether the cited evidence supports the proposition.
- **Asserted with no evidence, as agreed background.** Legitimate, and common in documents written for an audience that shares the background. Check that the party the document is arguing against would in fact grant it, rather than taking the document's word that it is agreed.
- **Asserted with no evidence, load-bearing, and not agreed.** A defect, and a reportable one. It is an element introduced because nothing forbids it, so classify it as category (c) under the classification of introduced elements and rule accordingly.

Then say where the supporting argument is claimed to live. A summary judgment is often the visible end of a process whose reasoning is recorded elsewhere, and a protocol that reports the summary as having no argument behind it has described the genre rather than found a defect. Name the place if the document points to one, note that it has not been evaluated here, and leave it as an open question rather than a finding. That concerns the missing derivation only — it does not excuse the warrant test above, which runs on what is in front of you.

## Stage 1 — Formal Logic

Treat the document's exegetical and textual premises as stipulated facts for purposes of this stage.

Do **not** ask whether the biblical passages have been interpreted correctly. Do **not** import Calvinist, Arminian, Provisionist, dispensational, covenantal, or other theological systems. Do **not** judge the argument by consequences you think it may have beyond its stated claim.

Ask only:

1. What exactly is the stated conclusion?
2. What are the premises necessary to reach it?
3. If all stated premises are true, does the conclusion follow?
4. Is there any non sequitur, equivocation, circular reasoning, illicit property transfer, composition/division fallacy, hidden premise, or modal shift?
5. Can you construct a countermodel in which every premise is true but the stated conclusion is false?
6. Does an objection actually deny the stated conclusion, or does it concede the conclusion while disputing some further question?

**On countermodels.** A countermodel succeeds only if it grants every premise the argument actually asserts — including its explicit interpretive identity premises — and still makes the conclusion false. Denying an exegetical premise that Stage 1 is required to stipulate is not a countermodel. It is a Stage 2 objection arriving early.

**On hidden premises, distinguish two kinds.** A *logical bridge premise* is genuinely required for validity and its absence is a Stage 1 defect. An *exegetical identity premise* is an interpretation of the texts; if the document states it, it is not a formal defect, and it belongs to Stage 2.

**On scope, be strict, and be strict in both directions.** If the claim is "election to salvation," do not require the argument to prove "unconditional election to salvation" unless the document itself claims that. And if the document's argument establishes a conclusion over a narrower class than its thesis sentence quantifies over, report the gap rather than passing the stage. A valid proof of a narrower proposition is not a proof of the stated one.

**On redundancy.** For every alleged defect, state whether the affected premise or route is necessary to the conclusion, one of several independent sufficient routes, corroborative only, or irrelevant to the proof. Do not declare a conclusion defeated by the failure of a redundant argument. And do not accept "independent sufficient route" on the document's say-so: check that the surviving route does not rest on the premise that just fell, and state what it reaches without the one that did.

For every alleged flaw, identify the exact premise or premises and the conclusion involved, and show formally why the inference fails.

End Stage 1 with one of these verdicts:

- Formally valid.
- Formally invalid, with the exact invalid inference identified.
- Formally valid only if an unstated bridge premise is supplied; state that premise exactly.
- Formally valid for a narrower conclusion than the one stated; state the narrowest conclusion the premises support, and state what the stated conclusion adds beyond it.
- Not an argument — the stated conclusion is asserted rather than derived. Name the asserted claims, and say whether the document offers or points to an argument for them elsewhere. This verdict is not a failure and must not be reported as one. Neither does it clear the claim: it says only that there is no inference for this stage to check, and the warrant for the assertion is tested at Stage 2.

Do not proceed from "this has controversial theological implications" to "therefore the argument is invalid."

## Stage 2 — Exegesis of the Cited Texts

Now test the premises that Stage 1 treated as stipulated.

The question is not whether the theology is congenial to some system, but whether the cited texts support the propositions attributed to them.

For each important exegetical premise:

1. State exactly what proposition the argument claims the text establishes.
2. Classify the kind of claim being made:
   - **direct statement** — the text says it;
   - **same-context inference** — inferred within one passage;
   - **same-author identification** — one writer's usage read through another of his letters;
   - **canonical synthesis** — texts from different books joined;
   - **systematic inference** — beyond textual synthesis into theological construction.
   Then ask whether the document represents its own move accurately. For example, "Isaac and Jacob are heirs with Abraham of the same promise" is nearly a direct statement; "Hebrews' city is Revelation's New Jerusalem" is canonical synthesis. Both may be sound, and their evidentiary standing differs.
3. If it is an inference, ask whether it is necessary, strongly supported, plausible but contestable, weak, or contradicted by the context.
4. Examine the immediate literary context, argumentative flow, repeated vocabulary, pronouns, categories, contrasts, and stated conclusions.
5. Where several passages are linked, determine whether the proposed identity or continuity is warranted rather than assumed.
6. Construct the strongest competing reading that still respects all the textual data.
7. Compare explanatory power: which reading accounts for more of the actual wording with fewer unsupported partitions or imported distinctions?
8. Distinguish a **possible** alternative from a **better-supported** one. Conceptual possibility is not enough to defeat an exegetical argument. The no-conceivability-repair rule under the general referee rules governs how this is applied, and it governs both readings.
9. Do not let a theological system decide what a text is allowed to mean.

**On continuity across a passage**, do not accept the absence of an explicit change of subject as the argument. Look for positive markers: repeated terminology, repeated categories, inclusios, inferential conjunctions, stated conclusions, and the question the author set himself.

**On partitions.** The burden here is conditional on the paragraph above, and the two rules must be read together. Neither continuity nor partition gets a presumption from being conceivable. Continuity must first be established by positive markers of the kind just listed; once it has been, the party proposing a partition bears the burden of locating it in the text. This covers partitions of one covenant into different contents for different named recipients, one promise into unrelated fulfillments, one class into different populations, one repeated expression into unrelated senses, and one argumentative sequence into separate subjects. A partition is not established by being conceivable.

**Say which relationship the markers establish.** Repeated vocabulary can mark identity, analogy, or continuity in one respect. Evidence of analogy does not by itself establish identity. Continuity in one respect does not by itself establish continuity in every respect. So state the particular relationship the markers support, and extend the partition burden only that far. The same rule applies to a claim of discontinuity: it must state what its own markers establish.

**On explicitness.** Do not treat "the text does not say this in so many words" as an objection by itself. Most arguments contain inference. The question is whether the inference best explains the textual data and whether a competing reading explains them better.

This paragraph and the no-conceivability-repair rule under the general referee rules are easy to mistake for opposite instructions, and the mistake is the one that rigs a review. This paragraph says an inference is not defeated by being unstated. That rule says an inference is not established by being unstated either. They are the same standard seen from two sides, and it is comparative explanatory adequacy in both cases. So an evaluator may not forgive the document's unmarked moves under this paragraph while charging the critic's unmarked moves under that rule. Whichever of the two he invokes, he owes the same accounting to both readings.

**On redundancy**, as in Stage 1: say whether a failed premise is necessary or belongs to a route the conclusion can spare.

End Stage 2 with:

- which premises are textually very strong;
- which are strong but inferential;
- which are genuinely contestable;
- whether any necessary premise fails;
- and whether, on balance, the exegetical premises required by the Stage 1 proof should be accepted.

## Stage 3 — Canonical Stress Test: Cited and Uncited Context

Stages 1 and 2 evaluate what is on the page. This stage asks what the rest of Scripture does to the argument — in both directions.

**This is a retrieval task before it is a critical one.** Do not begin by criticizing the document. Assemble first; assess second.

Search four categories:

- **A.** Passages apparently against the conclusion or a necessary premise.
- **B.** Passages that materially support the conclusion and are not used.
- **C.** Passages that qualify or limit a subsidiary claim without touching the conclusion.
- **D.** Omitted immediate context of passages the document does cite — the rest of a verse, the following clause, the parallel in another Gospel.

Category D matters as much as A. For example, a document that quotes Romans 8:17a and stops has not engaged 8:17b, and an opponent will finish the sentence.

Then, for each passage:

1. State in one sentence what an opponent would claim it shows, or what it contributes if supportive.
2. Mark whether the document addresses it, mentions it without answering, or omits it.
3. For those not addressed, determine whether it contradicts a necessary premise, contradicts the conclusion, is compatible once its own context is examined, or bears on a bracketed question.
4. Where it is compatible on examination, say what reading makes it compatible and whether that reading is natural or strained.

**Rank every omission** on this scale rather than calling it material or not:

- **Fatal if unanswered** — contradicts the conclusion or a necessary premise, with no independent route surviving.
- **Material** — seriously weakens a load-bearing premise.
- **Qualifying** — requires narrower wording, leaves the proof intact.
- **Bracketed** — bears on a question the document has explicitly declined to settle.
- **Neutral** — commonly cited in this debate but does not bear on the stated thesis.

**Rank supportive passages on the same scale rather than admitting them in one undifferentiated class.** An evaluator with five graded tests for discounting an attack and one bucket for admitting support will produce a lopsided report without intending to, so grade both sides:

- **Supportive and load-bearing** — establishes or independently reaches a necessary premise, and its absence is a real omission.
- **Supportive and corroborative** — strengthens the case without being needed by any route.
- **Supportive but redundant** — reaches only what a cited passage already reaches, and adding it lengthens the document without strengthening it.
- **Supportive only for a bracketed question** — bears on something the document has declined to settle, so it cannot be used to make the stated thesis look better supported than it is.
- **Commonly cited for the system but not reaching the thesis** — the mirror of Neutral. Do not credit a passage as support merely because it is customarily marshalled for the position the conclusion is associated with. Ask what it does for the stated conclusion.

**On scope of retrieval.** Include the standard biblical texts used to deny the stated conclusion or one of its necessary premises. Do not collect proof-texts that attack only downstream doctrines the document does not claim. For example, if a document argues that election terminates in salvation and brackets the basis of election, texts about the necessity of faith are not counterexamples; they bear on a bracketed question.

**Do not count a passage as damaging merely because it is commonly aimed at the system the conclusion is associated with.** Ask what it does to the stated conclusion. The same holds in reverse: do not count a passage as supportive merely because it is commonly cited for that system. Both habits substitute the system's reputation for the thesis's content, and an evaluator who applies only the first will discount attacks while waving support through.

**Where a passage damages the thesis sentence but not the proof.** Say so in those words, and give both results. A counterdatum that reaches only the width the thesis sentence added beyond what the argument establishes is a scope finding, and the remedy is a narrower thesis rather than a defeated one. Report it as: what the datum defeats, what survives it, and the narrowest statement of the thesis the datum leaves standing.

**Close with the whole-canon countermodel test.** Ask whether there is a coherent reading of all the relevant texts, cited and uncited, on which every necessary textual datum is both **affirmed and adequately explained** while the thesis is nonetheless false.

The wording matters. Affirming a verse is cheap — a countermodel can grant every sentence in isolation while draining each of the force it carries in its context. The test is explanation, not consistency.

To keep that honest, build a **datum ledger** before stating the countermodel:

1. Enumerate the load-bearing textual data the argument rests on. List them as data, not as the document's conclusions from them.
2. State the countermodel.
3. For each datum, say whether the countermodel **naturally explains it**, **accommodates it only by adding a distinction or partition**, **qualifies its apparent force**, **leaves it unexplained**, or **contradicts it**.
4. Name every partition or added distinction the countermodel requires, and assess its textual warrant. State each introduced element as the exact proposition the reading needs, applied to the case in dispute, and classify that proposition by the three-way scheme under "Classification of introduced elements." Do not compare costs until every element a reading cannot do without has been classified (a) or (b). This applies to the document's reading and the countermodel alike. A distinction with positive or independent warrant may carry a cost. A merely conceivable distinction that the countermodel cannot do without disqualifies it; it is not a cost the countermodel can bear.
5. Do the same for the document's own reading. Name every distinction or explanatory level the document supplies that the text does not mark, and charge it on the same scale. A ledger that charges only one side is not a comparison.
6. Account for **patterns**, not only for individual verses. Where several data points form a repeated structure, a countermodel that addresses each one separately has not yet explained the structure. For example, one prenatal designation may be called incidental; two successive prenatal designations that both resolve into the same inheritance line are a pattern, and the countermodel owes an account of it.

Then compare symmetrically: identify every datum the countermodel explains less naturally than the document's reading does, and every datum it explains better. Say which reading carries the lower explanatory cost, and by how much.

If no coherent countermodel survives that test, say the canonical countermodel fails. If one does, say so plainly — a surviving countermodel means the thesis is not exegetically unavoidable, which is different from its being defeated.

End Stage 3 with:

- the passages the document should address and does not, each ranked;
- the supportive passages it should add;
- whether any omission is fatal;
- whether any omission is a scope finding rather than a defeat, and the narrowest thesis it leaves standing;
- the datum ledger and the partitions the countermodel requires;
- and the result of the whole-canon countermodel test.

## Stage 4 — Systematic/Theological Consequences

Only after Stages 1 through 3 are complete, assume the conclusions that survived them and ask what follows theologically.

1. State the narrow conclusion actually established.
2. Identify theological positions it directly rules out.
3. Identify positions it places under pressure but does not refute.
4. Identify further doctrines that would require additional arguments.
5. If a theological opponent can preserve his system only by conceding the argument's conclusion and moving the dispute to a new issue, say so explicitly.

**Classify every downstream claim** as one of:

- **Entailed** — follows with no additional substantive premise.
- **One-premise consequence** — follows once one clearly stated additional premise is supplied. State the premise. For example, election to salvation plus the necessity of saving faith yields the certainty that the elect will believe.
- **Pressure only** — makes another position harder to maintain without contradicting it.
- **Compatible** — fits the conclusion but receives no support from it.
- **Unresolved** — neither supported nor excluded.

**Do not smuggle the next argument backward.** Do not criticize the present argument for failing to establish a downstream proposition merely because that proposition would be needed for the author's eventual larger position. If the present argument deliberately establishes only premise A of a later A-plus-B argument, judge A on its own.

End Stage 4 with:

- the immediate theological consequence;
- the strongest further consequence and the premise it requires;
- what the argument does **not** yet establish;
- and which theological alternatives remain live.

## Standing of each thesis

End the review with the standing of each thesis the document claims. The standing adds no new judgment. It is read off the results of Stages 1 through 3. Stage 4 may not raise or lower it.

- **Established.** The argument is valid at Stage 1. Every necessary premise is accepted at Stage 2. No omission at Stage 3 is fatal, and the whole-canon countermodel test fails.
- **Supported.** As for Established, except that a countermodel survives. The datum ledger charges it more than it charges the document's reading. The thesis is better supported than its strongest rival, and the rival is not excluded. A Supported standing must name the surviving countermodel, and every element it cannot do without must have passed the admissibility test at Stage 3.
- **Not shown.** The offered proof fails at a stated step and no independent route survives, or a countermodel survives at equal or lower cost.
- **Refuted.** A landed objection, after global propagation, establishes that the thesis is false.

**A thesis stands no higher than the imports it needs.** Where it has independent routes, the strongest route decides. A review that does not re-evaluate an import states the standing as conditional on it: *Established, given its imports.*

**Name the limiting step.** For any standing below Established, name the step, premise, import, or countermodel that keeps the thesis from the next standing up. A standing without a named limiting step is not a finding.

**Comparative theses.** Some theses say that one reading is better supported than another. A rival that survives at higher cost does not make such a thesis false. Only a reading on which the rival does as well or better does. So a comparative thesis is Established when the datum ledger shows the advantage and no admissible reading equalizes or reverses it. It is Not shown when an admissible reading equalizes the comparison, and Refuted when one reverses it.

**Name the deciding data.** A comparative finding, whether a comparative thesis or a Supported standing, must name the data on which the comparison turns and say which way each one cuts. A margin stated without its deciding data is not a finding.

**Grade the thesis as written and the narrowest thesis.** Where the pre-analysis found the stated thesis wider than the proof, give the standing of each.

Attack candidates do not lower a standing.

## General referee rule

At every stage, keep the proposition under review frozen.

An objection succeeds only if it attacks something the argument actually claims at that stage.

Do not strengthen the claim in order to defeat it. Do not weaken the claim in order to accept it. Do not use downstream theological discomfort as evidence that an upstream logical or exegetical inference is defective.

For every objection, take four steps in order:

1. **Identify the exact premise or inference the objection challenges.**
2. **Demonstrate the defect, and name the routes that need that premise or inference.**
3. **Ask whether another sufficient route survives without it.**
4. **Classify the result as one of three:**
   - **Refutation** — the objection establishes that the challenged proposition is false.
   - **Failure of the offered proof** — the objection establishes that the argument does not warrant its conclusion. It does not establish that the conclusion is false.
   - **Local correction** — the objection defeats or narrows a subsidiary claim or one route, and sufficient support for the conclusion remains.

Report only the result the objection establishes. Do not report a local correction as a failure of proof, or a failure of proof as a refutation. A defect in every offered route leaves the conclusion unproved by those routes. It does not make the conclusion false.

**On conceded readings.** When a document explicitly grants an opposing reading for the sake of argument and claims the conclusion survives the concession, evaluate whether the conclusion really does survive it. Do not spend effort deciding whether the conceded reading is correct, unless some other part of the proof depends on rejecting it.

### Proof objections and competing readings

These are two different things, and each carries its own burden.

**A proof objection** identifies an unsupported premise, an invalid inference, or a missing bridge in the document's argument. The evaluator must demonstrate the defect. He need not supply an alternative reading to do so.

**A competing reading** is a different account of the same evidence. It is a proof of its own. State its conclusion, its premises, its textual warrant, and its inferential steps. Test each step under the same standards applied to the document. Then weigh the two readings against the evidence.

Neither finding substitutes for the other. A failure in the document's proof does not establish a competing reading. A failure in the competing reading does not supply warrant the document's proof lacks. An evaluator who shows a gap in the document may not then report that a rival reading survives unless he has done the second task as well.

The same holds in reverse. A document that answers a competing reading has not thereby repaired a defect in its own proof.

### No-conceivability-repair rule

A reading may not be rescued by supplying an unstated premise, criterion, distinction, or explanatory level merely because the evidence does not explicitly deny it. The proposed element must have positive or independent warrant.

This applies with special force when the source itself states an argument, raises an objection that follows from that argument, and then answers the objection. A reading may not posit an unstated premise that would have dissolved the source's own objection, unless that premise is positively marked in the text or independently warranted elsewhere. The reason is that the source's actual answer is evidence about how the preceding argument is functioning: an author who had the dissolving premise in hand and answered instead by restating the disputed proposition has told you something about what he takes himself to have asserted.

Accordingly:

- Do not infer from silence alone that a possible criterion, distinction, or explanatory factor does not exist.
- Do not use a merely possible criterion, distinction, or explanatory factor to defeat a reading that the argumentative sequence positively supports.
- If a proposed unstated premise would naturally resolve an objection the source itself raises, and the source instead answers by restating, developing, or intensifying the disputed proposition, the reading requiring that premise incurs an additional explanatory cost. Enter the cost on the ledger.
- A countermodel survives Stage 3 only if the distinctions, criteria, or explanatory levels it requires are positively or independently warranted, not merely compatible with the evidence.

**This rule is symmetric, and applying it in one direction only is a misuse of it.** It is stated over readings, not over critics. The document under review supplies unstated distinctions and explanatory levels of its own, and every clause above applies to those on the same terms. An evaluator who charges the countermodel for its unmarked elements and passes over the document's has not run the test; he has built a shield, and under that shield no document can fail. Before closing any stage, name the unstated elements the document's own reading requires, and say whether each is positively warranted, independently warranted, or merely conceivable.

**The symmetry is enforced at each ruling, not only in a summary at the end.** An evaluator can satisfy the paragraph above while rejecting six attacks one at a time and writing the symmetry report afterward as a formality. So: whenever an attack is rejected because one of the elements it requires is unmarked, identify in that same ruling the corresponding elements the document's own reading requires, and state why each of those has stronger warrant. If that comparative showing cannot be made, the attack may not be rejected for being inferential.

### Classification of introduced elements

Whenever an attack, a defense, or a countermodel is saved by introducing a distinction, premise, criterion, or explanatory level stated neither by the document nor by the cited source, stop and classify that element explicitly as one of:

- **(a) positively warranted** — the source marks it;
- **(b) independently warranted** — it is established elsewhere on evidence the reading can cite;
- **(c) merely conceivable** — nothing establishes it, and it is introduced because the evidence does not forbid it.

Category (c) cannot make an attack succeed and cannot make a defense succeed. Say which category each introduced element falls in before ruling on the objection it serves. Do not rule first and classify afterward.

**Argue the classification; do not announce it.** This is where an evaluator's leverage is greatest, because moving one element from (b) to (c) decides the objection it serves without any further argument. So give the reasoning each time. If a critic offers a passage as independent warrant for his element, the evaluator may not label the element merely conceivable and move on. He has to say what relation that passage does warrant, what relation the countermodel needs, and why the second does not follow from the first. The same duty applies to an element of the document's own.

**The duty runs the other way too.** An element is (b) only if the evidence establishes the relation the reading needs, applied to the case in dispute. A relation shown to hold elsewhere does not warrant its application here until the application is argued. For example, a condition stated in one person's appointment does not show that a differently worded appointment of another person carries the same condition. Before classifying an element as (b), state the proposition the reading needs, what the cited evidence establishes, and the inference that connects them. Apply this to the document and to its rivals alike.

### Ground versus undisclosed reason

Denying that a conclusion, outcome, or determination is grounded in a specified kind of factor does not entail that it is reasonless, unexplained, or arbitrary.

Distinguish a **reason why X obtains** from a **qualifying condition on which X is contingent**. A position may deny that X is contingent on a qualifying condition of some specified kind while leaving open that other reasons, purposes, explanations, or considerations account for X.

Two consequences follow, and they run in opposite directions.

First, an objection does not defeat a no-grounding claim merely by observing that some further reason could exist, or by producing one. It must show that the reason it produces stands in the grounding relation the claim denies. A reason of some other kind is not a counterexample.

Second, an undisclosed qualifying condition may not be introduced merely to preserve a countermodel. If the countermodel needs such a condition, the condition requires positive or independent warrant, and the classification above applies to it.

### Referee check

When evaluating an attack, ask:

> Does this attack identify evidence supporting the premise or distinction it needs, or does it merely introduce something the evidence could conceivably leave unstated?

If the latter, the attack fails unless the proposed premise or distinction is independently warranted.

Then ask:

> Does the alleged reason actually stand in the grounding relation the argument denies, or is it some other kind of reason or explanation?

A reason defeats a no-grounding claim only if the relevant grounding relation is established.

Then ask the same two questions of the document's own defense of itself, and report both results.

### Adversarial-strength rule

The evaluator must make the strongest admissible case against every load-bearing premise and conclusion before accepting it.

Do not test the argument only against obvious, common, or easily answered objections. Construct the strongest objection the agreed corpus supports. State it in the form its most capable advocate would use. Then test the document's strongest answer. Then construct the strongest rejoinder to that answer. Do not stop because the document has anticipated an objection.

Where several attacks are available, prefer the one which, if it succeeded, would defeat the most. Where a published or otherwise well-developed opposing reading states the objection better than the evaluator first did, use that version.

**The evaluator's effort must be hostile; his standards must not be.** An attack is inadmissible if it changes the thesis, denies a premise stipulated at that stage, imports authority outside the agreed corpus, rests on mere conceivability where warrant is required, demands proof of a bracketed proposition, or borrows consequences belonging to a later stage.

A premise passes only when its positive warrant has been assessed and the strongest objections the evaluator has identified have been tested through the document's strongest answer and the strongest rejoinder to it. No evaluator can certify that no stronger objection exists, so state what the search covered and what it did not. Surviving criticism does not replace positive warrant.

> Do not ask whether the document has an answer. Ask whether its answer survives the strongest answer to the answer.

### What counts as a landed objection

An objection exists only when the evaluator states a warranted contrary proposition and identifies exactly which load-bearing premise or conclusion it defeats. A question is not an objection. A possibility is not an objection. An undeveloped inference is not an objection.

The form is:

> Text or premise P positively warrants proposition Q. Q contradicts or undercuts load-bearing proposition R. Therefore R fails or requires revision.

Where Q contradicts R, the result is a refutation. Where Q undercuts R's support, the result is a failure of proof or a local correction. Classify it under the general referee rule.

Judge the objection by what it demonstrates, not by the verbs it uses. An objection whose decisive step is a possibility asserted without support is an **attack candidate**, not a landed objection. Report it as one. An attack candidate may be worth recording — it tells the author where to look — but it does not count against the document and must not be summed with landed objections in any verdict.

What counts as demonstrated depends on the claim attacked:

- Against a claimed entailment, a countermodel that grants every stipulated premise and makes the conclusion false is demonstrated, even when it is stated as what could be the case.
- Against a claim that one reading is better supported, a possible alternative does nothing. The objection must show that the comparison itself is inadequately supported.
- Against a claim that the corpus warrants a proposition, the objection must show which step the cited text does not license.

State which of these the objection does. Do not report one as another.

### Global propagation

An objection that works locally may still fail once the rest of the argument and the rest of the evidence are held in view. A reading of one clause can be available locally and false once the rest of its sentence, its book, and the canon are in view.

So before counting an objection successful, propagate it. Run it through:

- the relevant textual evidence, cited and uncited, whose warrant does not depend on the premise under challenge;
- propositions the document has established whose support does not depend on that premise;
- readings the document has conceded;
- questions the document has bracketed, unless the present proof needs them settled.

Then ask whether the countermodel the objection requires is still internally coherent, and whether it still contradicts the thesis.

**Hold fixed only what does not depend on the challenged premise.** Suppose the document argues from earlier texts to reading R, and from R to further conclusions. An objection to R must engage R's own support: the texts R was inferred from, the wording of the passage R reads, or evidence whose warrant does not run through R. It may not be rejected because it conflicts with conclusions derived from R, since those conclusions cannot establish the premise they came from. If a downstream conclusion has separate support, evaluate that support.

**Direction matters the other way too.** A defect in a downstream inference does not by itself refute R. The fault may lie in the later step, and repairing that step may leave R standing. Where R, the inference from R, and a downstream conclusion cannot all be held together with the evidence, the argument as written fails. Say which repairs are available — deny R, deny the downstream conclusion and the inference to it, narrow the conclusion, or withdraw a hidden premise — and do not assign the fault until the evidence locates it.

An objection that survives locally and fails under propagation is not a finding. Say that it failed and say where.

This applies to the document's own defenses on the same terms. A defense that answers the objection in front of it while contradicting something the document established elsewhere is a defect, not an answer.

### Comparative support and categorical negation

Keep two questions apart.

**Is the alternative false?** Establishing that reading A is better supported than reading B does not by itself establish that B is false. A categorical conclusion needs contradiction, exclusion, or a further stated premise connecting evidential superiority to the negation. Do not let a comparative result be reported as a categorical one.

**Is the alternative admissible?** That is a different question and it has a different answer. An element with no positive or independent warrant in the agreed corpus is unavailable as a premise regardless of whether it is possible. It is not declared false; it is unrevealed by the corpus and therefore not something either party may build on.

The distinction matters in both directions. An evaluator who collapses them toward falsity turns an evidential rule into a metaphysical claim the evidence cannot support. An evaluator who collapses them toward possibility makes every argument defeasible by anything not expressly forbidden, which is the failure the no-conceivability rule exists to prevent.
