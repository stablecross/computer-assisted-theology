# Corpus

*Version 3.*

This file states the body of evidence the papers in this repository argue from. The Four-Stage Evaluation Criteria require a review to record the agreed corpus before anything else is assessed. A reviewer who does not accept this corpus should say so. A binding review stops there. A conditional review may go on, labeled as conditional: *given this corpus, the argument establishes X.*

## Authority

The corpus is the sixty-six books of the Protestant canon. Only these books are premises. Nothing outside them decides what a text means.

## Text

- **Quotations.** English quotations are from the New Revised Standard Version (1989). The NRSV Updated Edition (2021) differs in places and is not the text quoted.
- **Original languages.** Where an argument turns on the wording, the Hebrew of the Old Testament or the Greek of the New Testament is given, with a transliteration. Literal renderings of the original are set in italics and marked as literal.
- **The Septuagint and other ancient Greek.** These are cited only as evidence of how a Greek word or construction was used. They are not premises. The same holds for any passage from a book outside the canon, such as 1 Maccabees.

## Other sources

- **Confessions.** The Canons of Dort and the Westminster Confession are quoted to state what a tradition teaches. They are never premises. A paper that says its conclusion agrees with a confession is reporting agreement, not borrowing authority.
- **Modern scholars.** Grammarians, lexicographers, and commentators are cited for facts of grammar, usage, or background. Where a paper relies on one of them, it says what the citation supports. Where a citation is background only, the paper says it is not a premise.

## Method assumptions

The criteria require any method assumption the parties have not agreed to be recorded. The papers assume the following.

1. **One consistent whole.** Scripture gives very little guidance on how to read it. Christ read all the Scriptures as one whole, about himself (Luke 24:27). He could, because he knows the end from the beginning (Isa 46:10; Rev 22:13). We do not. So we cannot read the way he does, and we test our readings for consistency instead. These tests are assumptions, not findings.

   The papers assume that all of Scripture is a consistent whole. Each passage's claims are first established from its own language and discourse. A local claim is no more definite than its text. Where a passage leaves open who is meant, how far it reaches, or what is promised, that question stays open for the synthesis, and it is not settled by whichever text is read first. Once established, any warranted claim may constrain the synthesis of any other. One passage may clarify, expand, or otherwise bear on the reading of another only where the corpus warrants that relation. Thematic likeness, or the fact that two passages can be harmonized that way, is not enough. The final account must be consistent and must not depend on the order of reading: front to back, back to front, or from the inside out must produce the same result. A reading of one text that contradicts a warranted reading of another is not available; the criteria call this global propagation. When a paper uses a text from one book to read a text from another, it classifies the move as canonical synthesis and says so.
2. **Authorship.** Paul's letters are read together as evidence of one author's usage. The local context still decides what a word means in each place; Paul uses "flesh" for bodily life in Galatians 2:20 and for human nature set against God in Romans 8:8. Hebrews is not treated as Paul's. The common authorship of the Gospel of John, 1 John, and Revelation is treated as disputed, so joining them is marked as synthesis.
3. **The evidential rule.** A proposed factor may not serve as a premise merely because the text leaves it possible. It must be stated in the corpus, entailed by it, or warranted elsewhere in it. A factor without that warrant is not declared false. It is unrevealed, and neither party may build on it.

## How the papers are evaluated

Each paper is written to be tested under the latest Four-Stage Evaluation Criteria, which are in this repository. Reviews record the version of the criteria they used.
