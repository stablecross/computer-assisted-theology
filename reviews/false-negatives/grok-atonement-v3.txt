## Pre-analysis

The opening question says the paper argues that Christ died “for each and every human being,” against “only for the elect.” The claim block then states four theses, and they are not the same width.

The exclusion claim: the proposition “Christ died only for the elect” is false. Some for whom Christ died perish.

The extent claim: Christ died for every human being.

The bridge claim: every argument for limited atonement taken up here needs either an exclusion premise (“for the S” read as “for the S and no others”) or a bridge premise (whoever Christ died for is saved). Scripture states neither, and no argument taken up supplies either from a text.

The proclamation claim, as sentence: on limited atonement, the evangelist cannot tell an unbeliever anything true that saving faith could receive about what Christ’s death does about that unbeliever’s own sins. The apostles’ gospel tells him such a thing.

The elaborated proclamation claim, in the front matter and at steps 48 and 54, adds a third test the sentence does not contain: the proposition must be knowable before faith as a ground for believing, and it must not be “Christ died for you” under another name or “you will turn out to be elect.” Step 54 grants that “if you trust Christ for forgiveness, your own sins will be forgiven through his death” is true on limited atonement, concerns the hearer’s sins, and is what faith receives. That grant meets the sentence and fails only the added test. Widest statement and argument therefore diverge. Both are graded below.

Premises, by conclusion:

Exclusion rests on 2 Peter 2:1–3, the Jude 4 identification of the Master, and the identification of ἀγοράζω with the blood purchase. Hebrews 10:29 and John 11:51–52 are marked corroboration. The step from “they perish” to “they are not elect” is not an import from the other papers. It is the position under review’s own identification of the elect with those who are saved. The paper’s sentence that none of the imports is a premise of the exclusion claim holds.

Extent has two routes that share no textual premise. Route A has two lines, proclamation (steps 12 and 14) and commanded faith (steps 11, 13, and 14). Route B reads 1 John 2:2 with 5:19, and 1 Timothy 2:1–6. Step 21 is a further extension to those who died before the cross, carried by Hebrews 9:15 and Romans 5:18. The survival clauses in the paper are accurate: either route is sufficient for a universal conclusion, and the class narrows only if Route B falls and Route A remains.

Bridge is a claim about the arguments reconstructed in part four, not about every argument anyone has ever made. The scope sentence controls it.

Proclamation’s positive half rests on Acts 13:38 and 1 Corinthians 15:1–3. Its negative half rests on the tests of part five. The paper is right that it does not rest on Route A.

Non-load-bearing: steps 8, 9, 18, and 19; the historical notes on Dort and Westminster.

Bracketed: mechanism of the atonement, why anyone is passed over, the order of the decrees, and how the will works.

Conceded: limited atonement can command repentance and promise salvation to everyone who believes; “all” and “world” can be restricted in other texts; the Day of Atonement sacrifices never took away sins; δεσπότης can name the Father; an exodus acquisition that does not save is a real scriptural pattern.

Imports, and the use made of them. From the Abrahamic-covenant paper: the promise-heir class is elected to salvation, and believers belong to it. From the heart paper: the response does not occur while the heart of stone remains, and where it occurs God’s act brought it about. From the heirs paper: every heir is brought to faith, and no one justified finally falls away. These are used at step 32, step 34, and the objections “why elect?”, “then faith decides,” and “then everyone is saved.” They are not used to prove exclusion or extent. That boundary is kept. Step 34’s answer falls if the imports fall. The textual half of step 32 does not. The heirs paper’s greater-to-lesser rule is applied at step 38 as a rule about grounds, not as a conclusion smuggled across.

## Stage 1 — Formal logic

Exegetical premises are stipulated here.

Exclusion. If those men were bought by Christ by his death, and they perish, and perishers are not elect, then “died only for the elect” is false. That follows. The identities are stated premises, not hidden bridges. No countermodel grants them and keeps the conclusion false.

Extent, Route A. Each line is valid if its content premise is granted: that what is proclaimed cannot be true of a man not died for, and that the faith commanded includes an already-true “gave himself for me.” Those are exegetical premises, so they are not Stage 1 defects. The two lines do not need each other. Scope is stated: every lawful hearer, and the commission excludes no class.

Extent, Route B. If “the whole world” is everyone outside the believing company, and the ransom’s “all” is the same domain as the prayer for everyone, the universal conclusion follows. Step 21 is valid only if Romans 5:18’s second “all” names everyone the act was for rather than everyone who receives life. That is an exegetical premise. The paper’s use of 5:17 to block universal salvation is consistent with that premise and is not a modal shift.

Bridge. “Christ died for the S” does not entail “Christ died for the S only.” An argument of that form is invalid without an exclusion premise or a bridge premise. That is valid for the arguments as reconstructed. Whether a reconstruction omits a premise the published argument actually uses is not a formal question.

Proclamation. Relative to the three tests and the enumeration, the negative half follows. Exhaustiveness of the enumeration is a premise of that half. The positive half follows if Acts 13:38 and 1 Corinthians 15:3 assert, before reception, something about the hearer’s sins. No equivocation on “died for” appears inside a single inference; the one-sense policy is a stated term, examined where a conclusion turns on it.

Imports. Where they appear, they answer a consequence objection. They are not transferred into the exclusion or extent proofs. No illicit property transfer.

Stage 1 verdict: formally valid for all four claims. The proclamation claim is formally valid for the elaborated claim with the third test. The sentence without that test is wider than the argument, which is a scope gap recorded in the pre-analysis, not an invalid inference.

## Stage 2 — Exegesis of the cited texts

Exclusion, the load-bearing text. 2 Peter 2:1 states of the same men that the Master bought them and that they bring swift destruction on themselves. Finality is strongly supported in context: condemnation pronounced long ago (2:3), angels kept for judgment (2:4), cities condemned to extinction (2:6). Direct.

The Master is Christ. Canonical synthesis with Jude 4, marked by the same verb, the same noun, and the parallel structure of the two letters. The rival has the noun’s range. It has no marker in the passage. Better supported than the Father reading. The paper is also right that the Father reading would not save a purchase-entails-salvation bridge. It would, however, remove this verse from the exclusion claim. The Christ reading is the one the parallel supports.

“Bought” is the blood purchase. Canonical synthesis with Revelation 5:9 and 1 Corinthians 6:20 and 7:23. Peter does not name the price. This is the joint the paper marks, and it is the right one to press.

The exodus rejoinder is the strongest available. Scripture does contain a divine acquisition that does not save: Exodus 15:16, and Jude 5. Deuteronomy 32:6 does not establish the allusion. The Septuagint verb there is κτάομαι, not ἀγοράζω, and the parallels are “made” and “established,” with birth language at 32:18. The marked parallel in 2 Peter 2:1 is false prophets then and false teachers now, not the purchase. Carried to church attenders, the reading has to say what purchase those attenders stand in. 2 Peter 3:1 identifies the readers with the first letter, and 1 Peter 1:18–19 names the price as Christ’s blood. That does not place the teachers inside the ransomed “you.” It identifies the purchase Peter has in view when he writes of buying. The application of the exodus pattern to this verb is not warranted by the pattern’s existence elsewhere. The profession reading and the two-sense reading have no marker. Continuity is the shared situation, Christ buying men, once step 6 is granted. On balance the blood-purchase premise should be accepted. It is inferential, and it is better supported than the rivals that need an unmarked sense.

Hebrews 10:29 is corroboration, as marked. The subject of ἡγιάσθη is grammatically open. The paper’s reading, that the spurner was sanctified by the covenant blood, fits the clause’s place in the description of what he did. The Christ-as-subject reading is possible and unmarked. Not load-bearing. The shared reading with the heirs paper is a shared reading, not an imported conclusion.

John 11:51–52 is corroboration. Caiaphas’s ἔθνος at 11:48 is the nation. John’s “not for the nation only” distinguishes that object from the gathering of the children. John 10:26 has already said some of those Jews are not sheep. The political sense is Caiaphas’s; the prophetic sense is John’s. Strong as corroboration, rightly not made the carrier.

Route A, line 1. Acts 13:38–39 says forgiveness is proclaimed to a mixed audience, some of whom then reject it (13:46). Direct, as far as proclamation to them. The inference that the proclamation asserts “Christ died for each of you,” rather than announcing a forgiveness received by believing, is the weak joint the paper names. The next clause is “everyone who believes is set free.” The conditional reading is natural. Line 1 is plausible and contestable. It is not necessary if line 2 or Route B stands.

Route A, line 2. Galatians 2:20 states Paul’s faith: the Son loved him and gave himself for him. The aorists put the giving before the believing. Direct for Paul. The extension has two steps: that this is the faith of 2:16, and that every commanded hearer is commanded to believe an already-true “for me.” The first is same-context and reasonable. The second is the weak joint. A post-faith assurance reading explains 2:20 without that extension. Numbers 21 and John 3:14–15 support priority of provision to looking. They do not fix the class at every human being. Line 2 is contestable. Route A as a whole is the route the conclusion can spare.

Route B, step 16. 1 John 2:2 says the atoning sacrifice is “not for ours only but also for the sins of the whole world.” The contrast is direct: a class beyond the company written to. 1 John 5:19 uses ὁ κόσμος ὅλος against the same “we,” for those under the evil one. Same letter, same contrast. A reading on which 2:2 means the elect scattered through the nations has to give the phrase a sense it cannot have at 5:19. That partition is unmarked. The sufficiency reading, value rather than object, is resisted by “not only but also,” which extends the object. This is the strongest extent text in the paper. John 17:9 is correctly marked as synthesis, Johannine common authorship being disputed in the corpus file. The letter does not need it.

Step 17. 1 Timothy 2:1–6 repeats “all,” and the paragraph moves by γάρ from prayer for everyone, kings included without a believer-restriction, to the desire, to the ransom. Same-context inference, positively marked by the repeated phrase and the conjunctions. Restricting the ransom alone partitions an unmarked sequence. Strong.

Step 18 is corroboration, and the preserver reading has context on its side. Correctly not rested on.

Step 19. Second Corinthians 5:14–15 distinguishes “all,” for whom he died, from “those who live,” in a purpose clause. Both the subset reading and the purpose-for-the-living reading are available. Corroborative. Hebrews 2:9 is rightly not rested on.

Step 21. Hebrews 9:15 says the death redeems transgressions under the first covenant, for those called to the inheritance. It establishes backward reach for the forgiven. It does not by itself say every pre-cross person was died for. Romans 5:18 is the carrier the paper names. The parallel is direct. That the second “all” names everyone the act was for, rather than everyone who receives life, rests on 5:17. The federal reading, second “all” coextensive with those who receive, is a real competing reading of a parallel Paul builds. Genuinely contestable. Not necessary for the extent claim if steps 16 and 17 already name an unrestricted human domain. The paper’s own caution, that Route B’s verbs are present, is why step 21 is there. The aorist “gave himself a ransom for all” is not limited to the living after the commission.

The distinction the paper uses against bridge premises is positively marked. Second Corinthians 5:19–20 sets God reconciling the world, and not counting trespasses, beside an appeal to be reconciled. If those were the same act, the appeal would be empty. Romans 5:10–11 has reconciliation through the death and then reconciliation received. Romans 3:25 receives the sacrifice through faith. These are the paper’s own elements, and they have warrant. Romans 5:10 can be pressed the other way, since the “we” were reconciled while enemies. That shows reconciliation through the death for that company. It does not state the rule that everyone died for is thereby reconciled.

Part four’s reconstructions ask each argument for the premise that would be false if Christ died for everyone. That demand matches the form of the arguments taken up. Owen’s trilemma is loaded by the discharge premise. The double-payment argument is the same premise in judicial dress. Romans 8:32 is correctly singled out as the closest case. Paul’s “us” is the company of 8:28–31. Widening “gave him up for all of us, so everything else” into a rule for anyone died for carries a stated “us” past the paragraph that defines it. The use of the heirs paper is a use of the rule that an inference holds for anyone of whom its ground holds. It does not import the keeping claim as a premise about 8:32. Isaiah 53:6 and the apostolic use of 53:1 against unbelieving Israel (Romans 10:16, John 12:37–38) undercut the claim that sin-bearing and justification have the same objects. Step 32’s textual answer, that Philippians 1:29 does not say faith is granted to everyone died for, stands without the imports. The imports there only block a further consequence.

Stage 2 result. Very strong: the destruction clause; the 2 Corinthians 5:19–20 distinction; the 1 John 2:2 contrast; the 1 Timothy 2 sequence; “for” does not entail “only for.” Strong but inferential: Master is Christ; bought is the blood purchase; the nation at John 11:51. Genuinely contestable: Route A’s two content premises; Romans 5:18’s second “all” as extent of the act. No necessary premise of the exclusion claim or of Route B fails. On balance those premises should be accepted. Route A is not required.

1 John 2:2 with 5:19. The countermodel must partition a repeated phrase in one contrast. Unmarked. The paper uses one sense.

1 Timothy 2:1–6. The countermodel must restrict the prayer, the desire, and the ransom together, or split them. No marker. The paper follows the conjunctions.

2 Corinthians 5:19–20. The countermodel must take “world” as the elect, or treat the appeal as asking for what the death already completed in them. The appeal is the marker the paper uses. Advantage to the paper.

Romans 8:32. Closest datum. John 11:51–52. Partition of “nation” into the elect within it is unmarked. Caiaphas’s noun and “not only” cut against it.

Isaiah 53. A remnant-only “us” is available. The apostolic application of 53:1 to unbelieving Israel cuts the other way. Advantage to the paper, not decisive.

Galatians 2:20 and Acts 13:39. The countermodel explains these more naturally as assurance and as a conditional. Those data belong to Route A. Route B does not need them.

## Stage 4 — Consequences

The conclusions that survive are these. Some for whom Christ died perish, so “died only for the elect” is false. Christ died for every human being, on the domain fixed by 1 John 2:2 and 1 Timothy 2:1–6, with the pre-cross extension argued and contestable. The arguments taken up do not supply the premise they need. The elaborated proclamation claim stands as better supported than the answers surveyed.

Ruled out: limited atonement. Also ruled out, as a rule, the effect assumption that whoever was died for is thereby reconciled, and the purpose assumption that toward everyone died for the death’s purpose is his salvation and that this purpose does not fail. “Died for” does not entail “saved.” That follows with no further premise.

Pressured, not refuted: commercial satisfaction theories that identify death with discharge apart from reception; the claim that sacrifice and intercession have the same objects; the claim that the five points stand or fall together. The last is answered only given the imports.

One-premise consequence: extent, plus the paper’s own texts that not all come to life (John 5:28–29, Daniel 12:2), excludes universal salvation. The premise is in the paper.

Not established: the mechanism; why anyone is passed over; a full account of the death’s ends toward the non-elect; the order of the decrees. The certainty of the heirs’ faith and of final perseverance is not established here. It is imported, and the objections that universal extent would leave salvation to the man are answered only given those imports.

An opponent who wants particular redemption can keep it only by defeating step 7 and the domain readings at steps 16 and 17. Conceding those and retreating to “sufficient for all, efficient for the elect” either grants the extent claim or fails the paper’s second test, as step 51 says. Hypothetical universalism, death for all and application to the heirs by God’s act, is the position this paper and the imports occupy together. This paper does not re-argue the application.
