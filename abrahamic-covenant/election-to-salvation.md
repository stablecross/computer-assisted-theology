# Election in the Abrahamic Covenant Is to Salvation

*Version 40.*

**Claim under review.** Election in the Abrahamic covenant — **the designation of the promise-heir class, with Isaac as the founding case and those Paul places in his category as its later members** — is election to salvation. Isaac was named before he existed as the one with whom the covenant would be established. Paul then puts believers in Isaac's category, so the claim covers the children of the promise, not just Isaac and Jacob in Genesis.

The qualifier in the claim sentence is doing work and should not be dropped in summary. Scripture uses election language of corporate Israel in places this argument says nothing about — Deuteronomy 7:6–8 and Isaiah 41:8–9 among them, and Romans 11:28, where Paul calls Israel enemies as regards the gospel and beloved as regards election, for the sake of their ancestors. The claim does not reach those. What is under review is the naming of the heir.

**Not under review.** Whether that election was conditioned on foreseen faith. Whether grace in producing faith is resistible. Total depravity, particular redemption, perseverance. Whether every person who stood in the marked Abrahamic community was elect — Ishmael, the wilderness generation, and the broken branches are all distinguished below.

**What "to salvation" means here.** Salvation is the intended terminus of the election — where the naming lands, not merely a benefit available within the community the named man enters. The distinction matters. Suppose God names ten men into a covenant in which salvation is promised to whoever believes, and five believe. All ten were named into a covenant containing salvation. It would not follow that ten were elected to salvation. This argument claims the stronger thing.

**What an objection has to do.** One of two things. It can show that the covenant designation did not have salvation as its terminus. The opponent may grant it every other content — vocation, lineage, inheritance rights, covenant privilege — and win by showing that salvation was available within what Isaac was named into rather than being what he was named to. That is a refutation. Or it can show that a step below does not establish what it claims. That is a failure of proof: it leaves the terminus unproved, not disproved, and the objector need not prove the opposite.

Two live positions, and only one of them is an objection.

*Not an objection:* that God named Isaac because he foresaw his faith. That agrees about the terminus and disputes the basis. It is a different argument, taken up elsewhere.

*An objection:* that Isaac was named into a covenant in which salvation is offered, without the naming determining that he would receive it. This is the common view and the real opponent. It is met twice below. Step 8 argues that it cannot be held together with premises it already grants. Steps 9 through 13 then show that the class Paul constitutes terminates in salvation. The claim does not deny that faith is the means by which what was promised is received — step 8 turns on that being so.

**What the argument does not import.** No theory of the will. No account of how responsibility attaches to an act. No claim about what would be fair. No definition of freedom, and no position on whether a determined choice can be a free one. The steps below run on who was named, what was promised, who received it, and where Scripture places the men afterward. An objector cannot answer any of them by adjusting his view of volition, because none of them consults it.

**What it does use.** Two things, neither of them metaphysics. First, that a promise stated as God's own act, made to a named man, is not left unpaid to that man — grounded at step 3 on God's own oath and inability to lie rather than on a principle about promises in general. Second, a rule of reading: a designation covers what was just defined unless the text supplies a partition. That is a canon of construction, stated at step 8 and argued there.

**The spine.** The inheritance was promised to Abraham personally. He died without receiving it. Hebrews locates the patriarchs' fulfillment beyond their mortal lives. Jesus places Abraham personally in the eschatological kingdom. So Abraham's own promised inheritance is received after his death, in resurrection.

**How the parts depend on each other.** Two routes reach the conclusion. The main route runs steps 1 through 13: the personal grant, its indefectibility, reception after death, the scope of the inheritance, the patriarchs in the kingdom, Isaac named into the same covenant, then across to the class. The appendix reaches the same place through Romans 9, by arguing that the class Paul introduces at 9:8 is the class he describes at 9:23–26.

Each route has premises the other does not use. So a defect in either route's own premises leaves the other standing.

Both routes need one proposition: **believers belong to the Abrahamic promise-heir class.** If that fails, both lose their crossing, and what survives is a claim about Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob.

Two things support that proposition. Galatians 4:28 states it with Isaac named — children of the promise, like Isaac. Galatians 3:29 states it without naming him, and Hebrews 11:9 puts Isaac on the same side of that description.

The second of those is not independent of the main route. It carries the crossing only if *promise* at Galatians 3:29 is the grant of steps 1 through 8, and that identity is argued at step 12. So step 12 holds up the main route and one of the crossing's two supports.

Where each of these can be attacked is set out at the end, under what is inferred and where to attack.

## The argument

**1. God promised Abraham two things, personally.**

"I will establish my covenant between me and you, and your offspring after you throughout their generations, for an everlasting covenant, **to be God to you** and to your offspring after you. And I will give **to you**, and to your offspring after you, the land where you are now an alien, all the land of Canaan, for a perpetual holding; and I will be their God." (Gen 17:7–8)

Two clauses, both naming Abraham himself alongside his offspring: the land, and the God-relation. Both matter, and they fail differently under objection.

**2. Abraham died without receiving the land.**

*Which promise is at issue.* Abraham receives several distinct promises, and they do not all stand in the same condition at his death. He is promised offspring numberless as the stars (15:4–5), and they come. He is promised a son by Sarah specifically (17:15–16, 19), and Isaac is born. He is promised the land in the covenant ceremony of 15:18 and again at 17:8, and that one he does not receive. This argument concerns the land grant, personally to Abraham, and nothing in it turns on promises that were discharged in his lifetime.

"He did not give him any of it as a heritage, not even a foot's length, but promised to give it to him as his possession and to his descendants after him, even though he had no child." (Acts 7:5) Stephen names both parties and reports that the first got nothing.

Abraham did hold one piece of the land, and he bought it. He asked the Hittites for it as "a stranger and an alien residing among you": "give me property among you for a burying place" (Gen 23:4). He paid four hundred shekels of silver (23:16), and the field and the cave "passed from the Hittites into Abraham's possession as a burying place" (23:20).

The Hebrew noun is the one God used in the grant. God promised the land "for a perpetual holding" (17:8), אֲחֻזַּת עוֹלָם, *ʾăḥuzzat ʿôlām*. What Abraham bought is called אֲחֻזַּת־קֶבֶר, *ʾăḥuzzat-qeber*, a holding for a grave (23:4, 20). The holding God promised was his to give. The holding Abraham had, he paid for, and it held his dead.

Stephen also speaks of "the tomb that Abraham had bought for a sum of silver" (Acts 7:16). So the speech that reports ground Abraham bought also reports that God gave him none of the land. Ground bought is not the heritage given.

Hebrews says the same of all of them: "All of these died in faith without having received the promises." (Heb 11:13)

**3. The promise does not fail.**

Genesis calls the covenant everlasting and the holding perpetual (Gen 17:7–8). The Hebrew word is the same in both places, עוֹלָם, *ʿôlām*. God swore by himself, having no one greater to swear by (Gen 22:16). It is impossible for God to lie, and he confirmed the promise with an oath so that the heirs of the promise might see the unchangeable character of his purpose (Heb 6:17–18).

The Genesis 22 oath names offspring, enemies' gates, and blessing to the nations — not the land clause in so many words. So the indefectibility here rests on the everlasting language of the grant and on the character of the one who gave it, with the oath confirming his purpose toward the heirs.

Paul gives the same thing a word. The promise rests on grace, "in order that the promise may rest on grace and be **guaranteed** to all his descendants" (Rom 4:16) — βεβαίαν, made firm. And he says in that verse what it is guaranteed through: "For this reason it depends on faith." So the guarantee and the faith are not competitors in Paul's sentence. The promise is secured, and faith is the manner of its securing. Step 8 turns on exactly that arrangement.

The claim is narrow. It is not that every benefit of the covenant is unconditional for every party to it — Genesis 17 has covenant-breaking language in the same chapter, and Ishmael is circumcised and is not the heir. It is that what was promised to Abraham is not left unpaid to Abraham. A promise stated as God's own act, in terms he calls everlasting, is kept to the man it named.

**The clause at the head of the chapter.** Genesis 17 opens with a demand before it opens with a grant: "I am God Almighty; walk before me, and be blameless. And I will make my covenant between me and you, and will make you exceedingly numerous." (17:1–2) A reader may take the second sentence as the payoff of the first — be blameless, and then I will make the covenant.

Three things against that reading, and the first is the strongest.

The covenant was already made. Genesis 15 cuts it, ratifies it, and states the land grant, and chapter 17 comes later. So 17:2 cannot be the offer of a covenant that waits on Abraham's blamelessness, because the covenant exists before the demand is spoken.

The grammar does not print a condition. There is no *if*. Hebrew can state a condition plainly and does so elsewhere in the Pentateuch; this is an imperative followed by a cohortative, which can carry conditional force and need not.

And the content that follows is not offered in exchange. At 17:7–8 God states the land and the God-relation as what he will do, with no performance named as their price, and the paper's argument runs on those verses.

So 17:1 does what 17:9 and 17:14 do, which is govern how Abraham is to live within the covenant rather than whether the covenant will be given. That is the same distinction step 8 makes between standing in the marked household and being named to the grant. An objector may still press 17:1, and he should say why the demand conditions a covenant Genesis 15 already ratified.

**The obedience language, which an opponent will raise here.** Twice Genesis attaches the promise to something Abraham did. The oath at Moriah comes with "because you have done this, and have not withheld your son" (22:16), and God tells Isaac the grant is repeated "because Abraham obeyed my voice and kept my charge" (26:5).

Two things about those clauses. First, the content they attach to is not new. Land, seed, and blessing to the nations were all given at Genesis 12 and 15 and 17, before Moriah and before any of it. What Genesis 22 adds is an oath, not a term — God swears to what he had already said. Obedience is the occasion of the oath. The grant the oath confirms was given before it.

Second, Genesis 15 settles the form. The animals are cut, Abraham is put into a deep sleep, and the smoking pot and flaming torch pass between the pieces alone (15:12, 17). Abraham is unconscious and walks through nothing. A covenant a man sleeps through is not one whose ratification waited on his performance. Abraham's faith is reckoned to him earlier in the chapter (15:6), so the sleep does not show that faith played no part. It shows that the ratification waited on nothing Abraham did in it.

So the obedience clauses stand. They join the promise's fulfillment to Abraham's obedience, and the paper does not deny that. What they do not do is make the grant to Isaac uncertain, or restrict what Isaac was named to. A promise fulfilled through faith and obedience can still end in salvation.

One distinction within them, since they are not equally strong for the objector. Genesis 22:16 attaches obedience to an oath whose content was already given, so the answer above is direct. Genesis 26:5 is harder, because it can be read as a condition of the repetition to Isaac rather than of the original grant — God tells Isaac he is giving him these lands because Abraham obeyed. Grant the reading. It says the repetition was occasioned by Abraham's obedience. It does not say that the covenant God said at 17:19 he would establish with Isaac, spoken before Isaac existed, waited on anything Abraham would later do.

One clarification, because step 5 will otherwise seem to contradict this. The claim is not that Abraham must receive Canaan as surveyed. Hebrews says Abraham sojourned in the land of promise while looking for the city — he held the promise as pointing past the ground he walked on. So what is owed is what was promised as its recipient understood it, on Hebrews' account of him. The indefectibility is of the promise, not of a particular parcel.

**4. Therefore Abraham receives it after his death.**

Named recipient, unreceived in life, promise that does not fail. The reception is still outstanding when he dies, so it happens afterward.

That much follows. That it happens by resurrection does not follow from this step alone — a reader could posit a conscious post-mortem reception without a body. Step 7 supplies resurrection independently, from Jesus' own argument against the Sadducees, and nothing here needs to run ahead of it.

**What the conquest did and did not do.** An opponent will open here, and with texts rather than theory. "Thus the LORD gave to Israel all the land that he swore to their ancestors that he would give them... Not one of all the good promises that the LORD had made to the house of Israel had failed; all came to pass." (Josh 21:43–45; likewise 23:14 and 1 Kgs 8:56) And Nehemiah prays that God made a covenant with Abraham to give the land to his descendants, "and you have fulfilled your promise, for you are righteous." (Neh 9:7–8)

Both are true and the paper disputes neither. Two answers, and the second is the stronger.

The narrow answer: whatever Joshua discharged for Israel corporately, it did not give Abraham personally the possession Stephen says he never received. Abraham was dead four centuries before the crossing, and the balance Stephen reports is zero. Joshua can put Israel on the soil; he cannot close Abraham's books.

The fuller answer is about the instrument. Look at the terms on which Israel held what it received. The holding is conditioned on keeping the commandments (Deut 11:8–9, 22–25), and eviction is named in advance as the penalty for breaking them (Lev 26:32–33; Deut 28:63–64). Joshua says both things in one speech — not one word has failed (23:14), and if you transgress the covenant you will perish quickly from the good land (23:15–16). The exile is the record of those terms being met.

Now look at Genesis 17:7–8. God's own act, no conditions attached to the holding, and both the covenant and the holding called עוֹלָם, everlasting.

Two grants, two instruments, two sets of terms. What Israel received under Joshua it received and lost on Mosaic terms. That fulfilled the promise to the descendants historically. It does not exhaust a grant stated as everlasting and unconditioned.

**Paul says this in as many words.** "My point is this: the law, which came four hundred thirty years later, does not annul a covenant previously ratified by God, so as to nullify the promise. For if the inheritance comes from the law, it no longer comes from the promise; but God granted it to Abraham through the promise." (Gal 3:17–18)

And he says it again a chapter later, in the passage four verses before the one step 11 uses. Hagar and Sarah are "two covenants" — one from Mount Sinai, bearing children for slavery, and the other the free woman, whose child came by promise (Gal 4:21–31). Sinai and the promise are not one instrument on Paul's account. They are two, and he names them.

So the two are not one instrument. The later one does not annul the earlier, does not replace it, and does not exhaust it. And Paul frames the point in terms of the inheritance — the very thing at issue here. If the inheritance came by way of Sinai, it would not come by promise; it came by promise, so Sinai is not how it is given.

That also disposes of a formula that suggests itself naturally and is wrong. It is tempting to say the Abrahamic covenant combined with the Mosaic failed, and combined with the New succeeded. Neither half is right. The Abrahamic grant did not fail and was not part of the failure; Galatians 3:17 says the law did not annul it, and Jeremiah 31:32 says what was broken was the covenant made at the exodus. Nor does the New Covenant rescue the Abrahamic one. It is how the Abrahamic promise is administered and reaches its scope — the blessing of Abraham coming to the Gentiles in Christ, who is the seed to whom the promises were made (Gal 3:14, 16).

One promise, indefectible, made to Abraham and to the seed. Sinai came alongside for a time, was broken, and never annulled or replaced it.

That is not the same as saying the two never touched. Paul gives the law real work — added because of transgressions, a guardian until the seed came (Gal 3:19, 24). The claim here is only what Paul claims: that the later instrument did not void the earlier promise, and that the inheritance does not come by way of it.

Hebrews makes the same kind of move about the same conquest. "For if Joshua had given them rest, God would not speak later about another day. So then, a sabbath rest still remains for the people of God." (Heb 4:8–9) That concerns rest rather than the land clause, and the paper does not use it as though it settled the land. It shows that Scripture itself treats Joshua-era fulfillment as not exhausting what was promised — which is the shape this step claims for the inheritance.

The claim should not be overstated in the other direction either. Deuteronomy ties the conquest to the oath sworn to the fathers (Deut 1:8; 6:10), and the paper does not say the two are unrelated. It says that whatever relation the conquest bears to the Abrahamic oath, it was held on terms that could be and were forfeited, and the Abrahamic grant was not. So Joshua's sentence and Stephen's sentence are both true, and they are about different things.

Stephen says so himself, in the speech this step relies on. After reporting that Abraham got nothing (7:5), he says, "as the time drew near for the fulfillment of the promise that God had made to Abraham, our people in Egypt increased and multiplied" (7:17). So the exodus and the conquest fulfill the promise to the descendants historically. They do not exhaust the personal and eschatological fulfillment argued here, since the same speech reports that Abraham himself received nothing.

**The corporate objection, and why it does not close this off.** Someone will say the promise was made to a family and is paid when the family is paid — and Moses restates the oath as "To your descendants I will give it" (Exod 33:1; likewise Deut 34:4). But Stephen's sentence has both parties in it and reports a deficit to the first. A reader who takes the grant corporately has to say what Abraham himself was promised and what he got. And whatever he says about the land, the second clause is not open to that reading at all: no descendant can be the man whose God God is. A land grant can be received by a son on a father's behalf. "To be God to you" (Gen 17:7) cannot be satisfied by being God to somebody else.

**5. The inheritance is the world, and the city comes down into it.**

The obvious objection to what follows is that Hebrews replaces Abraham's earthly inheritance with a heavenly one — that the city is a different object, not the promised inheritance in its final form. Three texts close that off.

Paul states the scope of the promise, and the whole verse should be quoted: "For the promise that he would inherit **the world** did not come to Abraham or to his descendants through the law but through the righteousness of faith." (Rom 4:13) The faith clause is there, and it says how the promise comes — which is the question this paper brackets. What the verse gives here is the scope: the world. So the Abrahamic inheritance is not a parcel that the heavenly city would have to displace. Paul reads it as cosmic already.

Hebrews then gives the recipient's own expectation, and joins the two hopes in one sentence: "By faith he stayed for a time in the land he had been promised, as in a foreign land, living in tents... **For** he looked forward to the city that has foundations, whose architect and builder is God." (Heb 11:9–10) The staying as a foreigner and the looking are not rival objects. The second explains the first. "But as it is, they desire a better country, that is, a heavenly one. Therefore God is not ashamed to be called their God; indeed, he has prepared a city for them." (Heb 11:16)

A note on that clause, since step 5 and J15 both use it. The NRSV renders the connective as *indeed*, which strengthens the assertion without relating it to what precedes. The Greek is γάρ — causal — and the other standard versions translate it so: "for he hath prepared for them a city" (KJV), "for he has prepared for them a city" (ESV). The reading this argument uses is that the prepared city is the reason the title is not shameful, and that reading rests on γάρ, not on any version's English.

Hebrews then names the city, which shortens the reach to Revelation. "But you have come to Mount Zion and to the city of the living God, **the heavenly Jerusalem**, and to innumerable angels in festal gathering." (Heb 12:22) So the city introduced at 11:10 and prepared at 11:16 is not left unnamed by its own author. Hebrews calls it Jerusalem.

That matters for the step's weakest joint. The identification of Hebrews' city with Revelation's is an inference across two books, and the paper says so below. But it is an inference between two texts that use the same name for the same thing, not between a description in one book and a name in another.

And Revelation supplies the geography: "Then I saw a new heaven and a new earth... And I saw the holy city, the new Jerusalem, **coming down out of heaven from God**... 'See, the home of God is among mortals. He will dwell with them.'" (Rev 21:1–3, 10) The city does not stay in heaven and collect the patriarchs out of the world. It descends into the new creation.

**And four verses later John puts the two clauses of the Genesis grant in one sentence.** "Those who conquer will **inherit** these things, and **I will be their God** and they will be my children." (Rev 21:7)

Set that beside Genesis 17:7–8. There God says he will be God to Abraham, and will give him the land. Here, in the new creation, the inheritance and the God-relation arrive together, and a third thing is added: sonship.

The paper reaches those three separately — the inheritance at this step, the God-relation at step 7, the sonship at step 11. Revelation states them in one clause, at the place this step has been locating the consummation. That is corroboration rather than a premise, and it is worth having because it shows the three were not pulled together by this argument. They arrive together in the text.

So *heavenly* in Hebrews names the city's origin and builder, not a destination that removes Abraham from the inheritance he was promised. Canaan, world, and city are one line: the promised inheritance, read at its full scope by Paul, expected past death by Abraham, and located in the renewed creation by John.

**One more text, which the paper cites elsewhere and an opponent will turn here.** Galatians 4:26 says the free woman "corresponds to the Jerusalem above; she is free, and she is our mother" — and step 4 uses that passage for its two-covenant argument. A reader may say Paul has located the promise-covenant's object *above*, which is the heaven-versus-earth contrast this step denies. The answer is Revelation 21 again: the Jerusalem above is the city that comes down. *Above* names where she is from and whose she is, as *heavenly* does in Hebrews. Paul's contrast at 4:25–26 is between the present Jerusalem in slavery and the free one, not between a place off the earth and a place on it.

Jesus takes up the same inheritance language. The psalm says the meek shall inherit the land (Ps 37:11); he says the meek shall inherit the earth (Matt 5:5). The English looks like an expansion, and the step does not lean on that appearance: the Septuagint and Matthew both have γῆ, which carries land or earth, so the Greek does not by itself widen anything. Romans 4:13 supplies the scope. Matthew 5:5 corroborates that the inheritance language is still in use and still unbounded by Canaan's borders.

**6. Jesus places the three men in the kingdom.**

"I tell you, many will come from east and west and will eat with Abraham and Isaac and Jacob in the kingdom of heaven, **while the heirs of the kingdom will be thrown into the outer darkness, where there will be weeping and gnashing of teeth**." (Matt 8:11–12) Three men, long dead, at the meal.

The second half is quoted because the sentence does not end at the comma, and an opponent will finish it. The heirs of the kingdom are thrown out. Luke has the same saying with both halves in one clause: "There will be weeping and gnashing of teeth when you see Abraham and Isaac and Jacob and all the prophets in the kingdom of God, and you yourselves thrown out. Then people will come from east and west, from north and south, and will eat in the kingdom of God." (Luke 13:28–29)

So the argument has to say who the excluded heirs are. They are the ones addressed — Israelites by descent, presuming on the standing descent gave them, which is the distinction Paul draws at Romans 9:6 when he says not all Israelites truly belong to Israel. The exclusion clause therefore separates descent-standing from the promise-class rather than telling against the promise-class. That is the same separation step 8 makes with Ishmael, who held the sign and was not the named heir.

And notice what the clause does for the step. If descent-heirs can be cast out while Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob sit at the meal, then the three men are not there on the strength of descent. Their presence turns on something descent does not supply.

The banquet is not a title-transfer ceremony, and the argument does not need it to be. What it shows is that Abraham personally is present in the eschatological kingdom — which is where step 5 has already located the fulfillment of the promise he died without receiving. Steps 4, 5, and 6 together give the reception: still outstanding at death, located by Hebrews past mortal life and in the prepared city, and the man himself placed there by Jesus.

**7. Jesus reads the God-relation as entailing their life.**

Jesus names the subject before he gives the argument: "**And as for the resurrection of the dead**, have you not read what was said to you by God, 'I am the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob'? He is God not of the dead, but of the living." (Matt 22:31–32, citing Exod 3:6) The formula is corporate on its face — it is how God identifies himself to Moses about a nation. What individualizes it is Jesus' own conclusion, which is about the three men severally.

**Luke reports the same exchange with more of the reasoning showing, and the step is stronger from his version.** "Those who are considered worthy of a place in **that age** and in **the resurrection from the dead**... cannot die anymore, because they are like angels and are **children of God**, being children of the resurrection. And the fact that the dead are raised Moses himself showed, in the story about the bush, where he speaks of the Lord as the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob. Now he is God not of the dead, but of the living; for to him all of them are alive." (Luke 20:35–38)

Three things sit in that passage that Matthew's version leaves implicit. The subject is named as the age to come and the resurrection, not merely the resurrection. The ones who attain it are called children of God. And Luke adds the clause Matthew does not have: *to him all of them are alive.*

So the God-formula is not being read as a figure of speech about a nation's history. Jesus reads it as a statement about three men who are alive to God, and he draws the resurrection from it. That is what step 7 needs, and Luke prints more of it.

So the second clause of Genesis 17:7 is still in force when Jesus speaks. And Hebrews tells you what that relation held for them: he is not ashamed to be called their God, and a city is prepared.

**8. Isaac was named into the same covenant before he existed.**

*The premise this step supplies:* when God says he will establish his covenant with Isaac, the designation covers the covenant as defined eight verses earlier — inheritance and personal God-relation together — unless the text supplies a partition limiting the naming to part of it. The paragraph below is the case that no such partition is given.


"My covenant I will establish with Isaac, whom Sarah shall bear to you at this season next year." (Gen 17:19, 21) Said while Sarah was barren and Isaac unconceived. God repeats the grant to Isaac himself: "to you and to your descendants I will give all these lands, and I will fulfill the oath that I swore to your father Abraham." (Gen 26:3) He repeats it to Jacob, with the blessing of Abraham named (Gen 28:3–4, 13–15). Exodus calls it God's "covenant with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob." (Exod 2:24; see 6:4–5)

One covenant, three men, no second grant introduced. Hebrews states it outright: Abraham lived in tents in the land he had been promised, "as did Isaac and Jacob, who were **heirs with him of the same promise**." (Heb 11:9) Not heirs of a promise resembling his. The same one.

And the three end in the same place. Abraham buys the cave at Machpelah as a burying place (Gen 23:4, 20). Jacob also buys ground: at Shechem "he bought for one hundred pieces of money the plot of land on which he had pitched his tent," and he built an altar there (Gen 33:19–20). So the patriarchs did hold land they had paid for.

It did not count as receiving the promise. Hebrews says Isaac and Jacob lived "in tents" in the land they had been promised, "as in a foreign land" (Heb 11:9). Jacob had pitched his tent on ground he owned, and Hebrews still counts it as living in a foreign land. And Hebrews says all of them "died in faith without having received the promises" (11:13), Jacob with the rest.

The ground they bought became graves. Isaac is buried at Machpelah. Jacob owned a field at Shechem, and he still charged his sons to bury him in Abraham's cave, naming Abraham and Sarah, Isaac and Rebekah, and Leah as already in it (Gen 49:29–31). Joseph made his brothers swear, "you shall carry up my bones from here" (Gen 50:25), and his bones were buried "at Shechem, in the portion of ground that Jacob had bought" (Josh 24:32). Hebrews counts Joseph's charge as faith: "By faith Joseph, at the end of his life, made mention of the exodus of the Israelites and gave instructions about his burial" (Heb 11:22).

Joshua adds that Jacob's plot "became an inheritance of the descendants of Joseph" (Josh 24:32). The descendants inherited the ground. The patriarch was buried in it. That is the distinction step 4 draws between the promise fulfilled to the descendants and the promise to the named man.

So all three named recipients of the covenant lie in ground bought in the land they were promised, and none of them received the land. The grant lands the same way for each: unreceived, and awaiting.

**Both men are designated before birth, and the difference is in the form of the announcement.** Isaac is named directly: my covenant I will establish with Isaac, spoken before he is conceived. Jacob is designated by the inheritance formula: "the elder shall serve the younger," spoken before either twin is born and before either has done anything (Gen 25:23).

That formula is not merely a forecast of political subordination. The oracle does speak of two nations and two peoples, and one stronger than the other; that national horizon is in the text and the paper does not deny it. In Genesis it is the reversal of firstborn precedence — the younger takes the place birth order would have given the elder — and Genesis proceeds to cash it out as inheritance: the birthright at 25:29–34, the blessing at 27, Isaac's conferral on Jacob of "the blessing of Abraham" at 28:3–4, and God's own confirmation of the same land promise at Bethel.

So the sequence is the same for both. Prenatal designation, then historical conferral, then direct divine confirmation. And Hebrews names them together afterward: Isaac and Jacob, heirs with Abraham of the same promise (11:9). In both cases the identity of the Abrahamic heir is settled before the heir can act.

**Therefore: the covenant election of Isaac was election to salvation.** He was named, before he existed, into a covenant whose land clause is received in the kingdom and whose God-relation Jesus treats as holding past death.

The objection to face is not "two covenants." Nobody says that. It is that one covenant can carry several things, and that the prenatal naming took in the vocational ones while Isaac's saving participation came later by faith. That objection needs a partition inside the naming — Isaac designated to some of the covenant's content and not to the rest of it.

Genesis 17 does contain lines of a sort. Verse 9 requires the covenant to be kept. Verse 14 cuts off the uncircumcised male, who has broken the covenant. So the chapter distinguishes standing in the marked community from something else. But that is not the partition the objection needs, and Ishmael shows why: he kept the sign in full, on the day it was given, and was still not the one with whom God said he would establish the covenant. The sanction governs who remains inside the marked household. It does not govern who is named.

What Genesis does not supply is a line inside the naming itself. God defines the covenant in 17:7–8, personal God-relation included, and in 17:19 says he will establish *that* covenant with Isaac. The proposed partition is a possibility, and it is unsupported by the speech that defines the covenant.

**The other half of that objection is not an independent escape once the naming's full scope is granted.**

The objection has two halves. One is that the naming took in only part of the covenant, which is the partition just answered. The other is that Isaac's saving participation came later, by faith, so that the naming did not settle it.

What follows shows that the second half cannot be held once the first is given up. It is a conditional proof, and its condition is this: grant the objector's best case, that the naming covered everything 17:7–8 defines, and the later-faith move is no longer available to him. An objector who wants to keep it has to go back and win the partition, which is the paragraph above, or deny that the promise is indefectible to Isaac personally, which is the second exit named below.

Four things are then on the table, and the objection in its usual form grants all four.

First, the promise to a named man is not left unpaid to that man. Step 3 argues this of Abraham, from the everlasting language of the grant and the character of the one who gave it.

Second, Isaac is a named recipient of that same promise, not of a resembling one. Genesis 17:19 says God will establish *that* covenant with him, and Hebrews 11:9 calls him heir with Abraham of the same promise. So whatever indefectibility attaches to the grant attaches to it in Isaac's case, because it is the same grant and he is named to it.

Third, what was promised includes the land at its full scope and the personal God-relation — step 1 for the two clauses, step 5 for the scope, steps 6 and 7 for where Scripture locates the reception.

Fourth, faith is the means by which what was promised is received. This paper grants that, and the objection is built on it.

Now put them together. If Isaac does not believe, then Isaac does not receive what he was named to, because faith is how it is received. If Isaac does not receive what he was named to, then a promise stated as God's own act to a named man has gone unpaid to that man. Therefore Isaac's believing is not an open question left for later. It is settled by whatever settles the inheritance, and the same texts settle both.

Which means that *later by faith* states the order in time and nothing further. The faith does come after the naming. It is not thereby made contingent on something the naming left unsettled, because on the objector's own grants the naming entails that the faith occurs.

The difference matters. The claim is not that the naming produces the faith or causes it. That would be an account of how the faith comes about, and this paper has no such account and does not need one. The claim is that the naming, together with the other two grants, entails that the faith is certain. A reader who holds that God named Isaac because he foresaw the faith can accept the entailment as stated, since he agrees the two go together and disputes only which explains which.

So the usual form of the objection cannot be held together. It wants three things at once: that the naming covered the grant whose end is the kingdom; that the promise cannot fail to Isaac; and that Isaac's reception waited on whether he happened to believe. The first two are steps 1 through 8. The third is the ten-men model from the front matter. The third cannot sit on top of the first two.

**Abraham reasons this way himself, and the text says so.** "By faith Abraham, when put to the test, offered up Isaac. He who had received the promises was ready to offer up his only son, of whom he had been told, 'It is through Isaac that descendants shall be named for you.' **He considered the fact that God is able even to raise someone from the dead** — and figuratively speaking, he did receive him back." (Heb 11:17–19)

Follow the reasoning Hebrews attributes to him. The promise is attached to Isaac by name. Isaac is about to die. The promise attached to him cannot fail. Therefore God must be able to raise him.

That is the same shape as the argument above, run on a different obstacle. Abraham does not conclude that the promise to Isaac might lapse if Isaac dies. He concludes that death cannot be what defeats it, and he concludes it because of what a promise naming Isaac is. What the promise overrides depends on what it names. At Moriah it named descendants through Isaac, so the obstacle it had to overcome was his death. Whether it also overrides his not yet believing depends on whether the promise to Isaac includes his own reception of the inheritance. Steps 1 through 8 argue that it does, and Hebrews 11:19 cannot supply that premise.

This is corroboration and not a premise. The entailment above stands on its three grants without it. What Hebrews 11:19 adds is that the inference from *named in the promise* to *the obstacle must give way* is one Scripture attributes to Abraham rather than one this paper invented.

**What this does not claim.** It does not say why God named Isaac. A reader who holds that God named him because he foresaw the faith agrees that the naming and the faith go together and disputes which explains which. That is the question of basis, and the front matter puts it outside this paper. What is shown here is that the faith is not left open by the naming — which is the terminus claim, and the thing the objection was denying.

**What the objector has left.** Three exits, and he must take one. He can deny that the naming covered the salvific content, which is the partition, answered above. He can deny that the promise is indefectible to Isaac personally. Or he can deny that reception requires Isaac's own faith, which abandons the objection, since the objection is built on faith being the means.

**The second exit has a best text.** Romans 9:6: "It is not as though the word of God had failed." An objector will say Paul concedes there that named members of the covenant people can be lost while God's word still stands, and that Isaac's reception can be left open on the same terms.

Read what Paul says next, because he explains himself in the same sentence: "For not all Israelites truly belong to Israel, and not all of Abraham's children are his true descendants." He saves God's word by *restricting the class* — the promise was never made to everyone descended from Israel, so their loss is not a default on it.

Then notice who he names as the restricted class's first case. The sentence continues: "but 'It is through Isaac that descendants shall be named for you.' This means that it is not the children of the flesh who are the children of God, but the children of the promise are counted as descendants." (9:7–8) So Isaac is not in the population 9:6 is explaining. He is the man Paul names when he says which population it is.

Notice also which sentence Paul quotes to do it. *It is through Isaac that descendants shall be named for you* is Genesis 21:12, and it is the same sentence Hebrews 11:18 puts in Abraham's mind at Moriah. The one text an objector reaches for to keep Isaac's reception open is a text whose own proof runs through the clause naming Isaac.

Therefore aiming 9:6 at Isaac aims a class-restriction at the founding member of the restricted class. It is the one man in the chapter it cannot be used against.

**The same exit has a second text, from Abraham's own story.** "No, for I have chosen him, that he may charge his children and his household after him to keep the way of the LORD by doing righteousness and justice; so that the LORD may bring about for Abraham what he has promised him." (Gen 18:19) An objector will say the fulfillment waits on the household's obedience, so a promise to a named man can go unpaid if the obedience fails.

Read the order of the clauses. God chose Abraham so that he would charge his household. The household keeps the way of the LORD so that the LORD may bring about what he promised. The verse joins fulfillment to obedience, which this paper grants. It also makes the obedience the purpose of the choosing, not a term the choosing waits on. That is the order of "he chose us in Christ before the foundation of the world to be holy and blameless before him in love" (Eph 1:4).

So the verse does not show the fulfillment hanging on something God's choice left open. Whether that purpose can fail is a further question. The covenant answers it by saying who performs what it requires.

**The covenant says who performs what it requires.** Genesis 17 requires blamelessness and circumcision (17:1, 10–14). Moses carries the requirement to the heart. He commands it: "Circumcise, then, the foreskin of your heart" (Deut 10:16). Then he promises that God will do it: "the LORD your God will circumcise your heart and the heart of your descendants, so that you will love the LORD your God with all your heart and with all your soul, in order that you may live" (Deut 30:6). He ties that promise to the oath God swore to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob (30:20). So the covenant's own text commands the obligation and then puts its performance on God's side.

Paul says God performs it in everyone who believes. "In him also you were circumcised with a spiritual circumcision, by putting off the body of the flesh in the circumcision of Christ" (Col 2:11). The word the NRSV renders "spiritual" is ἀχειροποιήτῳ, *acheiropoiētō*, not made with hands. "It is we who are the circumcision, who worship in the Spirit of God" (Phil 3:3). And of the obedience itself: "it is God who is at work in you, enabling you both to will and to work for his good pleasure" (Phil 2:13).

So the obligations do not show a gap between being named heir and receiving the inheritance. In this covenant God undertakes what it requires, as a promise to the descendants of the oath (Deut 30:6) and, Paul says, in all who believe.

That reaches Isaac through his category. Paul puts believers in it by name (step 9): "you, my friends, are children of the promise, like Isaac" (Gal 4:28). For the children of the promise, the requirement is performed by God. An objector who holds that Isaac alone had to supply it himself is proposing a partition inside the category Paul built on him, and he has to find it in a text.

**The guarantee is made to the seed called in Isaac.** Paul says the promise depends on faith "in order that the promise may rest on grace and be guaranteed to all his descendants" (Rom 4:16). The objector reads that as a guarantee to a class and not to Isaac. But in the same letter Paul says which seed it is: "It is through Isaac that descendants shall be named for you … the children of the promise are counted as descendants" (9:7–8). The noun is the same in both places, σπέρμα, *sperma*, and so is the promise. So the seed to whom the promise is guaranteed is the seed called in Isaac. A guarantee to that class reaches Isaac, because his naming is what defines it.

**Two comparisons remain.** Solomon was chosen as God's son, and God said he would establish his kingdom forever on condition that Solomon kept his commandments (1 Chr 28:6–7). The condition is stated there. "My covenant I will establish with Isaac" (Gen 17:19) states none. Hebrews says the heirs inherit "through faith and patience" (6:12). In the same paragraph it says God swore an oath "to show even more clearly to the heirs of the promise the unchangeable character of his purpose" (6:17). The faith and the guarantee stand in one paragraph there, as they do in Romans 4:16.

So the texts brought for the second exit warrant something other than what it needs. Each of them either puts the requirement on God's side or, read in its own context, joins the requirement to the guarantee. None of them says that Isaac's naming left his reception open. What remains for the exit is the possibility that Isaac might not have believed. Under the evidential rule stated in corpus.md, a possibility is not a premise.

And the rest of the objection meets what step 4 already established against a corporate discharge for Abraham. No descendant can be the man whose God God is. Hebrews puts Isaac under the same promise and the same non-reception in life. Jesus puts Isaac himself at the table.

The first two attack a premise. The third withdraws. What is not available is a fourth thing, in which Isaac is named to the whole grant and might still miss it.

## How it carries past the three men

**9. Paul puts believers in Isaac's category by name.**

"Now you, my friends, are children of the promise, like Isaac." (Gal 4:28)

That is the shortest bridge between the two halves of this argument, and it is Paul's own sentence rather than an inference from one. The category was built in Genesis 17 and named in Romans 9:8 — the children of the flesh are not the children of God, but the children of the promise are counted as descendants. Paul takes that category and says believers are in it, with Isaac named as the pattern.

He says the same thing in the other direction a chapter earlier: "And if you belong to Christ, then you are Abraham's offspring, heirs according to the promise." (Gal 3:29)

Steps 10 through 13 take the longer route — singular seed, adoption, co-heirship — and that route establishes more than this one sentence does. If this sentence holds, believers are in the category whose founding case steps 1 through 8 have been arguing about. If it fails, Galatians 3:29 with Hebrews 11:9 still reaches the same proposition, as the note in the front matter says, so what is lost is one support and not the crossing.

The objection to expect is that *like* means resemblance rather than category membership — that believers are called children of the promise by analogy with Isaac rather than placed in his class.

**Paul answers it himself, three verses later, and drops the comparison while doing it.**

"But just as at that time the child who was born according to the flesh persecuted the child who was born according to the Spirit, so it is now also. But what does the scripture say? 'Drive out the slave and her child; for **the child of the slave will not share the inheritance** with the child of the free woman.' So then, friends, **we are children, not of the slave but of the free woman**." (Gal 4:29–31)

Two things there. Paul brings inheritance into the contrast — one child inherits, the other does not — which is the very thing at issue and not a point about the manner of birth. And then he states the placement with no comparison word at all: *we are* children of the free woman. Not like them. Are.

So the thin reading cannot rest on the word *like*. It has to go on and say why Paul raises inheritance at 4:30 and states the placement without a comparison at 4:31, and it has to say what that inheritance is if it is not the promise this paper has been tracing.

**What this step takes the inheritance at 4:30 to be.** The thin reading is charged with selecting one feature of a passage that supplies more. This step makes a move of its own: it takes the inheritance Paul raises at 4:30 to be the grant of steps 1 through 8, rather than the standing that the free-and-slave contrast is about on its own. That is an inference and not a printed sentence. What supports it is that 4:30 quotes an inheritance text, that Galatians 3:29 names believers heirs according to the Abrahamic promise, that Hebrews 11:9 puts Isaac under that same promise, and that Romans 4:13 gives the promise its scope. Those are reasons, and they are not the same thing as Paul saying it. An objector who confines 4:31 to the allegory's own contrast is arguing against those reasons, and he is not merely conceding a possibility.

The appendix on the transfer of properties reinforces this from a different direction, by collecting the covenantal properties Scripture reassigns to believers one at a time. That is cumulative support behind a direct statement, not the primary defense.

**10. The promise was made to Abraham and to one seed, who is Christ.**

"Now the promises were made to Abraham and to his offspring; it does not say, 'And to offsprings,' as of many; but it says, 'And to your offspring,' that is, to one person, who is Christ." (Gal 3:16)

So the singular heir of the Abrahamic promise is Christ. Whatever else is said about who inherits, it runs through him.

**11. Believers are adopted as sons, and the adoption was predestined.**

"God sent his Son, born of a woman, born under the law... so that we might receive adoption as children." (Gal 4:4–5) "For in Christ Jesus you are all children of God through faith... And if you belong to Christ, then you are Abraham's offspring, heirs according to the promise." (Gal 3:26, 29)

The timing is stated: "He destined us for adoption as his children through Jesus Christ, according to the good pleasure of his will" — and he "chose us in Christ before the foundation of the world." (Eph 1:4–5)

The NRSV's *children* renders υἱοί and υἱοθεσία, the Greek of sonship, and its *destined* renders προορίσας, predestined. This paper keeps *sons*, *adoption as sons*, and *predestined* for those words.

So what Ephesians names as predestined, before the foundation of the world, is adoption as sons.

**Adoption said of the unsaved, which is the hardest problem in this step.** Paul writes that to the Israelites "belong **the adoption**, the glory, the covenants" (Rom 9:4) — and he is grieving over those same Israelites as cut off from Christ. So *huiothesia* can be predicated of people who are not saved.

Sonship language makes it worse before it makes it better. Sonship is a covenant category before it is a Pauline one: God tells Pharaoh "Israel is my firstborn son" (Exod 4:22), and Hosea says "out of Egypt I called my son" (Hos 11:1). The son there is the nation. That national sonship did not save the nation.

So two facts stand together: Scripture calls Israel God's son, and Paul says the adoption belongs to Israelites he counts as lost. Any argument that moves from sonship to salvific inheritance has to get past both.

The answer is in the sequence Paul gives. "God sent his Son, born of a woman, born under the law, **in order to redeem** those who were under the law, **so that** we might receive adoption as children. And because you are children, God has sent the Spirit of his Son into our hearts, crying, 'Abba! Father!'" (Gal 4:4–6) Romans 8:15–16 says the same: the Spirit of adoption, bearing witness with our spirit that we are children of God.

And Paul says in the same letter where he grants Israel the adoption what the Christian adoption reaches: believers "groan inwardly while we wait for **adoption, the redemption of our bodies**." (Rom 8:23) Adoption in that sense terminates in the redemption of the body. The adoption of Romans 9:4 does not.

So the adoption of Galatians 4 and Ephesians 1 comes through redemption and is attested by the indwelling Spirit. Israel's sonship at Exodus 4:22 and Romans 9:4 is a standing within the covenant people. Same word, and Paul marks the difference by what the second one comes through and what accompanies it. The paper's claim is about the adoption Ephesians 1:5 names as the object of predestination, which is the one Galatians 4:5 reaches by redemption.

**The corporate reading of the *us*.** A reader may take Ephesians 1 corporately: God predestined the body in Christ to adoption without antecedently determining which persons would enter it. Grant it. The concession is sufficient here, because what the body is predestined *to* is adoption as sons — a salvific terminus, named in the verse. Whether the identities of the members are antecedently fixed is a further question and belongs to a further argument. This paper needs the terminus, not the roster.

**This concession is not the objection from the front matter.** The two look alike and are not. The objection was the ten-men model: named into a covenant where salvation is offered to whoever believes, so that membership does not itself land anyone in salvation. The corporate reading says something different — that the body is predestined *to adoption*, so membership in it is the salvific thing, and what is open is how a man comes to be in it. Granting the second does not grant the first. In the ten-men model the naming has a non-salvific terminus; on the corporate reading the terminus is adoption and only the roster is unsettled.

**12. What they inherit is the Abrahamic promise, held as co-heirs with Christ.**

Paul says it directly, in the same letter and the same argument as step 10: "And if you belong to Christ, then you are Abraham's offspring, **heirs according to the promise**." (Gal 3:29) Not heirs of something else on account of Christ. Heirs according to the promise — the one made to Abraham and to the seed, thirteen verses earlier.

**And 3:29 keeps 3:16's singular rather than undoing it.** The subject is plural — *este*, you all are. The predicate noun is singular — *sperma*, the same word and the same number Paul insisted on at 3:16. He does not say you are Abraham's seeds. The number alone would not show that a plural company is the one seed, since a collective noun can stand for many. The clause before it shows it: *if you belong to Christ*, εἰ δὲ ὑμεῖς Χριστοῦ. They are the seed by belonging to the one who is.

So the co-heirship of the next paragraph is not two parties holding adjacent shares of one estate. It is a plural group standing in the singular heir. That is why the object cannot come apart: there is one seed, Christ, and believers are that seed by being his.

Romans 8:17 then gives the form of the holding: "heirs of God and **joint heirs with Christ**." Co-heirs, of what he is heir of as the seed.

An opponent will finish the verse: "if, in fact, we suffer with him so that we may also be glorified with him." That names the path by which the heirs arrive at the glory they inherit. Grant the grammar its full reach: let *eiper sympaschomen* qualify the co-heirship itself and not merely its realization. It still specifies a condition on how the heirs reach co-glorification. It does not identify a different inheritance from the one Galatians 3:29 has already named — heirs according to the promise. Twelve verses later Paul gives the chain that loses no member between predestination and glory (8:29–30), in the same argument.

So the inheritance the adopted sons receive is not a parallel blessing with its own terms. That matters against the obvious objection to this route — that adoption confers a new-covenant standing unrelated to Genesis 17. Galatians forecloses it before it can be raised.

A thinner version survives. Someone will say the promise in view at Galatians 3 has been specified as the Spirit — "so that we might receive the promise of the Spirit through faith" (3:14) — and that what is co-inherited is standing, not the Abrahamic grant. Against that: Paul makes the seed Christ at 3:16 and the heirs those who are Christ's at 3:29 without introducing a second estate between them; Romans 4:13, same writer and same patriarch, names the promise as "the promise that he would inherit the world"; and 3:14's Spirit is how the Gentiles are brought into Abraham's blessing, not what replaces it.

**And the objection can be answered from the verses it has to reach across.** The attack says the promise-word was narrowed at 3:14 and stays narrow through 3:29. So look at what Paul does with it in between.

At 3:18 he uses a different word for the thing: "For if **the inheritance** comes from the law, it no longer comes from the promise; but God granted it to Abraham through the promise." Inheritance, granted to Abraham. That does not put the Spirit outside it. Paul calls the Spirit "the pledge of our inheritance" (Eph 1:14). The Spirit belongs to the promised inheritance. He is not evidence that the inheritance has become a standing.

At 3:22 the promise is again what believers receive: "so that what was promised through faith in Jesus Christ might be given to those who believe."

So between 3:14 and 3:29 Paul calls the promised thing an inheritance, and says God granted it to Abraham. An objection that the word stays narrowed has to account for the verse that widens it, in the same argument, eleven verses before the one it is attacking.

The attack is worth naming and is not worth conceding. It has a real hook at 3:14. It has to read past 3:18 to keep it.

**Peter says what the Abrahamic blessing is, to men who had it by descent.** "You are the descendants of the prophets and of the covenant that God gave to your ancestors, saying to Abraham, 'And in your descendants all the families of the earth shall be blessed.' When God raised up his servant, he sent him first to you, **to bless you by turning each of you from your wicked ways**." (Acts 3:25–26)

Peter quotes the Abrahamic promise and then names the blessing it speaks of. The blessing is being turned from wickedness.

That bears on this step because the objection here is about content. It says what believers inherit is a standing rather than the Abrahamic grant. Peter, addressing men who held the covenant by descent, tells them the blessing promised to Abraham's offspring is one that turns a man from his wickedness — which is not a standing, and not a piece of national furniture.

This is a second witness rather than a premise. The step stands on Galatians. What Acts adds is that an apostle preaching to the covenant people read the Abrahamic blessing as salvific in content, and said so in the same breath as the quotation.

**13. Which is where steps 1 through 8 have already been.**

Abraham was promised an inheritance he did not receive, which he collects after death, in the kingdom. Isaac was named into the same covenant before he existed. The adopted sons are fellow heirs of that same promise, and their adoption was predestined before the foundation of the world.

**Hebrews puts the two groups in one completion.** "Yet all these, though they were commended for their faith, did not receive what was promised, since God had provided something better so that they would not, **apart from us**, be made perfect." (Heb 11:39–40) The chapter has just listed the patriarchs among those who died without receiving. Its closing sentence says their completion does not come apart from the later company.

That is corroboration for what this step assembles rather than a premise it needs. What it corroborates is the joining: the men of chapter 11 and the *us* of verse 40 are not two stories with two endings. Hebrews says in one sentence that their completion does not come apart from ours.

**Therefore: the election of the Abrahamic promise-heir class is election to salvation.** Isaac is the founding case, named before conception into a covenant whose consummation is salvific. The adopted sons are fellow heirs of the same promise, predestined to that adoption before the foundation of the world. So the class is predestined to that adoption by God's purpose, and what it inherits is what steps 1 through 8 located in the kingdom.

That sentence is the whole claim, and not the wider one. It says nothing about every use of election language in the Abrahamic material, and nothing about corporate Israel. An objection that defeats the wider claim leaves this one standing.

## Supporting observations

**Entry into the kingdom requires a birth God performs.** "No one can see the kingdom of God without being born from above... no one can enter the kingdom of God without being born of water and Spirit." (John 3:3, 5) The kingdom where Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob are seated is not one a man enters by descent or by any birth he arranges for himself. The argument does not need this — steps 9 through 13 establish the class and its inheritance without it — but it is the same shape, stated as a rule.

**The promised child is announced before birth and produced where natural generation had failed.** Isaac, from a womb long past use (Gen 17:19; 18:11). Jacob, from Rebekah barren until the LORD was entreated (Gen 25:21, 23). John, from Elizabeth barren and advanced in years (Luke 1:7, 13). Jesus, from a virgin (Luke 1:31, 34–35). The word comes first; the birth follows by divine act.

This is corroboration, not a premise. Two cautions. Rebekah's conception produced Esau as well as Jacob, so a miraculous conception does not by itself mark out the promised child — the announcement does. And the virgin birth serves a christological purpose that is not simply another instance of the pattern. What the pattern shows is a shape that recurs: promise, human inability, divine action. John 3 states that shape as the rule for entering the kingdom. It does not say the barren wombs were types of regeneration, and this argument does not need it to.

**The covenant sign does not determine the heir.** God named Isaac — not Ishmael — as the one with whom he would establish the covenant, and said so in one speech (Gen 17:19–21). That same day Abraham circumcised Ishmael, named explicitly, along with every male of the household (Gen 17:23–26). So a man already passed over for the covenant received the sign in full. And Isaac received the sign at eight days old, by his father's hand (Gen 21:4). On neither side does the sign do the selecting.

## Two texts the paper does not need to answer

**Romans 11:17–22.** Branches broken off, others grafted in, and a warning that the grafted may be cut off too. An opponent will bring it as proof that Abrahamic membership is not an indefectible guarantee. He is right about that, and the paper does not claim otherwise: the naming of Isaac is not the same thing as participation in the covenant community, and step 8's support paragraph already separates them. Romans 11 concerns who stands in the tree. This argument concerns what Isaac was named to. For believers, steps 9 through 12 claim that those who belong to Christ are in the class and that the class is elected to salvation. Whether a present member can be cut off and finally lost is the question of perseverance, which the front matter brackets.

**Acts 7:45.** Stephen himself goes on to narrate Israel's later possession under Joshua. That is not in tension with 7:5. He reports a personal zero to Abraham and a later national occupation, in the same speech, without treating the second as settling the first.

## What is inferred, and where to attack

Steps 1 and 2 report what the texts say. Step 8 reports the naming and then adds two moves of its own, listed below with the rest. Every other step contains an exegetical move. Here they are.

**Step 3:** that the obedience clauses at Genesis 22:16 and 26:5 join the promise's fulfillment to Abraham's obedience without making the grant to Isaac uncertain or restricting its content, and that Genesis 17:1's "walk before me, and be blameless" governs life inside the covenant rather than conditioning its giving. The argument for this is that the content sworn at Moriah was already given, that Genesis 15 ratifies with Abraham asleep and precedes chapter 17 entire, and that no *if* is printed at 17:1–2. A reader may take any of the three clauses as conditional in force; he must then say what the oath added to what was already promised, and how 17:2 can offer a covenant Genesis 15 had already cut.

**Step 5:** three moves, not one. That Romans 4:13's "the promise that he would inherit the world" states the scope of the same promise Genesis 17 makes. That Hebrews treats the city as what the land-sojourn was pointing at rather than as a substitute object — Hebrews says Abraham sojourned in the land of promise *for* he looked for the city, but it nowhere says in so many words that the city is the final form of the Canaan grant. And that Revelation's descending city is the same city Hebrews names, which is an intertextual identification across two books. Each is defensible; none is stated. **Step 6:** that Abraham's presence in the kingdom is his presence at the fulfillment. **Step 7:** that the Exodus 3 formula continues the Genesis 17:7 clause. **Step 12:** that what Christ inherits as Abraham's singular seed is what the co-heirs inherit with him. Romans 8:17 names the co-heirship; Galatians 3:16 names what he is heir of; joining them is the step. **Step 11:** that the class is predestined to salvific adoption. Whether the identities of its individual members were fixed antecedently is not required, and step 11 grants the corporate reading rather than disputing it.

**Why step 9 is not on this list.** "Now you, my friends, are children of the promise, like Isaac" is a direct statement, not an inference. What is inferential is how much weight the word *like* carries — whether Paul means a full category identification or a looser resemblance.

That scope question is argued in step 9 itself, from Galatians 4:29–31, where Paul raises the inheritance and then says believers *are* children of the free woman without any comparison word. The appendix on the transfer of properties adds cumulative support. And Galatians 3:29 with Hebrews 11:9 reaches the same proposition by a route where *like* never appears, so an objector who wins the scope argument has not yet won the crossing.

The two supports fail to different first attacks. The attack on Galatians 4:28 is that *like* means resemblance. That attack has nothing to grip on Galatians 3:29, which uses no comparison word at all. An opponent who wants the crossing has to argue twice.

But neither support is sealed. Step 9's answer to the thin reading argues that the inheritance Paul raises at 4:30 is the Abrahamic grant, and it argues that partly from Galatians 3:29, Hebrews 11:9, and Romans 4:13 — which are the second support's own materials. And the second support carries the crossing only if *promise* at 3:29 is the grant of steps 1 through 8, which is step 12's argument. So an objector who breaks the object identity at step 12 reaches the second support directly and the first support's defense as well.

The premise is direct. Its scope is contested. And it is not the only support for what it supports.

The inferences below carry more weight than the rest, and an attack is better spent on one of them than on the list above.

**The indefectibility of the grant** (step 3). Everlasting language and God's character carry it; the Genesis 22 oath does not recite the land clause.

**Personal reception after death** (step 4). This stands on Stephen's two-party wording. A corporate reading has to say what Abraham personally was promised and what he got, and it does not touch the God-relation clause at all.

**The scope of the naming** (step 8). This is the hinge of the Isaac half. The argument's premise is that "my covenant I will establish with Isaac" covers the covenant as defined at 17:7–8, absent a textual partition. The opponent's counter-premise is that it covers a vocational subset. Neither sentence is printed in Genesis. What the text gives is the order: God defines the covenant, then names Isaac as the one with whom he will establish it, in one speech. The paper reads the naming at the scope of the definition it follows. A partition is available as a possibility and unsupported by the speech that defines the covenant.

**The certainty of Isaac's faith** (step 8, second half). This is an entailment rather than a reading of a text, and it should be labeled as one. From three things the objection grants — the promise does not fail to the man it names, Isaac is named to that same promise, and faith is how what is promised is received — it follows that Isaac's faith is not left open by the naming. The step imports nothing beyond those three. Its weakest joint is the second, since indefectibility is argued of Abraham at step 3 and carried to Isaac on the strength of Genesis 17:19 and Hebrews 11:9 saying it is the same covenant and the same promise. An objector who wants the conclusion gone has to cut one of the three. The objection in its usual form grants all three. An objector can instead contest the second, that the promise is indefectible to Isaac personally, and that is the second exit named at step 8. The texts offered for that exit are taken up there: Romans 9:6, Genesis 18:19, the obligations of Genesis 17 with Deuteronomy 30:6 and Paul, Romans 4:16 with 9:7–8, 1 Chronicles 28, and Hebrews 6:12.

Two limits on that entailment, both stated where it is made. It is a conditional proof: it grants the naming's full scope and shows what follows, so an objector who still wants the later-faith move must win the partition first. And it establishes that the faith is certain, not why — a reader who holds that God named Isaac because he foresaw the faith can grant the entailment and keep his account of the basis.

**The land-to-city objection** (step 5) is answered rather than conceded: Paul gives the inheritance a cosmic scope, Hebrews names its own city the heavenly Jerusalem, and Revelation brings a city of that name down into the creation. What remains contestable is whether Romans 4:13 glosses Genesis 17:8 in particular or summarizes the Abrahamic promises collectively. Either way the scope is the world, which is what the step uses. The cross-book identification of the two cities is still an inference, and Hebrews 12:22 narrows it rather than removing it.

**The singular seed** (step 10). Galatians 3:16 rests on the number of a noun — *sperma* rather than *spermasin*. A reader may say the Hebrew collective does not bear that weight. Paul makes the argument anyway, and the paper takes it from him; an objector is disputing Paul's reading of Genesis, not this argument's.

**What is co-inherited** (step 12). Galatians 3:29 names believers heirs according to the promise, and does so with a plural subject taking the singular *sperma* as predicate, on the condition that they belong to Christ — so an objector cannot make the co-heir's portion a different object from the seed's without emptying the co-, and cannot take 3:29 as retreating from 3:16's singular, since it uses it. What he can try is to shrink the object: that "promise" at Galatians 3 has been specified as the Spirit (3:14), so what is inherited is standing rather than the Abrahamic grant. Step 12 answers that three ways — from Romans 4:13, from the position of 3:14 relative to 3:16 and 3:29, and from 3:18, where Paul calls the promised thing an inheritance that God granted to Abraham, in the stretch the attack has to read across. Acts 3:25–26 corroborates from outside Paul, where Peter quotes the Abrahamic promise and names its blessing as being turned from wickedness. It is the one live attack on this step, and it reaches further than this step, because the second support for the crossing — Galatians 3:29 joined to Hebrews 11:9 — needs the same identity. So an objector who wins here wins twice. He now has 3:18 to get past first.

**The two adoptions** (step 11). Romans 9:4 predicates adoption of Israelites Paul counts as lost, and Exodus 4:22 has Israel as God's son. The paper distinguishes that standing from the adoption of Galatians 4:5 and Ephesians 1:5, on the ground that the second comes through redemption and is attested by the Spirit (Gal 4:4–6; Rom 8:15–16). A reader who takes the word univocally must then say what Galatians 4:6's sending of the Spirit adds to a status Israel already had.

**Predestination to adoption** (step 11). Ephesians 1:4–5 is corporate in address — *us* — and an objector may read it as predestination of a body rather than of named members. The paper grants that reading and does not need to defeat it: on either reading the class is predestined *to adoption*, which is the terminus this argument claims. What the corporate reading preserves is a question about how individual membership is determined, and that is not under review here.

One thing this argument does not settle: whether faith, graciously enabled, can serve as a condition on which God's choosing rests. "It depends not on human will or exertion" (Rom 9:16) excludes the man's willing as the source. Whether it excludes foreseen faith as a condition is a further question, and it is not answered here.

## Appendix: the transfer is not one property

*Corroboration for step 9, where the primary answer is given. Step 9 answers the thin-analogy objection from Galatians 4:29–31, in the immediate context of the sentence under attack. What follows adds a second kind of evidence from a wider field. It is placed here for length, not because it is spare: step 9 supports the proposition both this paper and its companion must cross on.*

**The objection.** Isaac has many properties and believers plainly do not share all of them. So "children of the promise, like Isaac" may transfer only one thing — the manner of coming to be, promise rather than flesh — which is what the surrounding allegory in Galatians 4 is about. On that reading the phrase is a comparison in one respect, and nothing follows about Isaac's designation.

That objection has real support. The Galatians 4 context is about Sarah and Hagar, free and slave, promise and flesh. A reader can say the likeness Paul asserts is the likeness that context is discussing.

**What would settle it.** A comparison transfers its point of likeness and stops. A category assignment carries the properties that define the category. So the question is whether Scripture treats believers as sharing Isaac's one salient feature, or as holding what made him the promise-heir.

**The evidence.** The covenantal properties are reassigned, one at a time, by name, in separate sentences, by different writers.

| Isaac | Believers |
|---|---|
| Child of promise | "Now you, my friends, are children of the promise, like Isaac." (Gal 4:28) |
| Born where natural generation failed | "born, not of blood or of the will of the flesh or of the will of man, but of God." (John 1:13) |
| Named before he existed | Names written in the book of life from the foundation of the world (Rev 17:8); "he chose us in Christ before the foundation of the world" (Eph 1:4) |
| Covenant established with him | "This cup that is poured out for you is the new covenant in my blood." (Luke 22:20) |
| Circumcised | "circumcised with a spiritual circumcision" (Col 2:11); the NRSV footnote gives the Greek as "a circumcision made without hands" |
| Inheritance | "heirs according to the promise" (Gal 3:29); "the promise that he would inherit the world" (Rom 4:13) |
| Offspring of Abraham | "And if you belong to Christ, then you are Abraham's offspring." (Gal 3:29) |

**The argument from that list.** Seven correspondences, each in its own sentence, across seven books. One correspondence is easily a comparison. Seven, distributed that way, make a one-feature reading substantially less economical — because the thin reading has to explain each of the other six separately, while the category reading explains all seven at once.

Notice what is not on the list. Believers are not Sarah's physical descendants, not bound on Moriah, not dwellers in Gerar. The properties Scripture reassigns are the covenantal ones and only those — which is what makes the pattern an assignment rather than a pile of resemblances. If the New Testament were reaching for illustrations it would have no reason to sort them this way.

And notice which single property the thin reading selects. It picks the manner of birth, which is one of the seven. The other six have nothing to do with how anyone was born. The reading that stops at one of seven has to say why six more were written.

**The cost.** Not all seven are identities, and this appendix does not claim they are. Isaac's birth was from a barren womb and the believer's is from God — the same shape, not the same event. Luke 22:20 is the new covenant in Christ's blood, not Genesis 17 repeated. Ephesians 1:4 and Revelation 17:8 are antecedent divine choice, not a name spoken to a man's father.

So this is not a proof that every property transfers at equal strength. It is evidence that the New Testament keeps handing believers the covenantal bundle rather than one feature of it, and that a reading confining "like Isaac" to one feature is choosing against that pattern.

**Where this sits.** Step 9's answer from Galatians 4:29–31 is direct: Paul raises the inheritance and then says believers *are* children of the free woman. This appendix is cumulative circumstantial support behind that sentence. It is good in that role and would be weaker standing alone.

*On the third row: Revelation 13:8 is often cited here, but its syntax is contested — "from the foundation of the world" may modify the writing of the names or the slaying of the Lamb. Revelation 17:8 has no such ambiguity and says what is needed.*

## Appendix: the same conclusion by way of Romans 9

*The main argument cites Romans 9, but it does not depend on this appendix's continuity inference. Romans 9 is where this dispute is usually conducted, and the chapter reaches the same place, so the route is set out here for readers who expect to find it.*

*What follows depends on one contested premise — that the class Paul introduces at 9:8 is the class he describes at 9:23–26. The main argument depends on no such continuity. A reader who rejects the premise below loses this appendix and keeps the paper.*

**R1. Paul constitutes the children of the promise through election, not merely through promise.**

"It is not the children of the flesh who are the children of God, but the children of the promise are counted as descendants" — Isaac the first case (Rom 9:7–8). Then the second case, and the reason for it: before the twins were born or had done anything good or bad, the word came to Rebekah, "so that God's purpose of election might continue, not by works but by his call." (Rom 9:11–12)

So "children of the promise" is not a label for people who later turn out to believe. Paul builds the category out of God's electing discrimination, and says so in those words. His conclusion from the two cases: "So it depends not on human will or exertion, but on God who shows mercy." (Rom 9:16)

**The problem Paul is answering is salvific.** At 9:3 he could wish himself accursed and cut off from Christ for the sake of his kinsmen. At 10:1 he names what he wants for them: that they may be saved. Those two verses fix what the question at 9:6 is about — how God's word can stand while so many Israelites are outside the salvation found in Christ.

They do not by themselves settle what kind of election Paul invokes at 9:11. A reader may grant that the problem is salvific and still say the Isaac and Jacob material is historical precedent, cited to establish God's freedom before that freedom is applied to salvation later in the chapter. That reading has to be answered from the argument itself, and R2 is where it is answered.

**The national texts are Paul's evidence, not his conclusion.** He grounds the second case on Genesis 25:23, which speaks of two nations, and on Malachi 1:2–3, which concerns Edom. Both are national, and an opponent will say the election Paul illustrates is therefore national. But Paul supplies his own gloss on the category, and it is not national: the children of the flesh are not the children of God, and the children of the promise are counted as offspring (9:8). *Children of God* is his phrase, added to the texts he cites rather than drawn from them.

Two further points about those citations, before the one that matters most.

The first is a correction of category. "The elder shall serve the younger" does describe subordination between the two peoples, but what it marks is the reversal of precedence — the younger takes the place birth order would have given the elder. That is inheritance language, and inheritance is what Genesis has been tracking since chapter 17. And in Malachi, "I have loved Jacob but I have hated Esau" (Mal 1:2–3) states God's covenantal preference for Jacob and his rejection of Edom; it is not a report of divine sentiment. So the pair of texts concerns who holds the covenant, not who was liked and who was assigned which job.

This does not settle what holding the covenant terminates in. That is the question of R2 and R3.

*On the love/hate language: the reading above is available from Malachi's own context, where the objects are Jacob and Edom as peoples under covenant. Readers who want the wider background will find the ancient Near Eastern treaty usage — where to love a suzerain is to keep his stipulations and to hate him is to break them — set out in Sandra Richter, "The Epic of Eden." That background is not a premise of this argument, which rests on the Malachi context alone.*

The second point is Paul's own practice, and it is the one that matters. Hosea's "not my people / my people" language is nationally framed — it concerns Israel's restoration. Paul applies it to "us whom he has called, not from the Jews only but also from the Gentiles" (9:24–26). So the national form of Paul's Old Testament source does not fix the level of his application. He shows this with Hosea, in the same argument, which means the opponent cannot infer from Genesis 25:23 and Malachi 1:2–3 being national that Paul's referent must be.

**R2. Paul carries one class through election, calling, mercy, and glory.**

The class is introduced as **children of God** and children of the promise (9:8). The Jacob case is explained by God's purpose of election continuing "not by works but by **his call**" (9:11–12). The conclusion is that it rests on God who shows **mercy** (9:16), and mercy continues as the subject at 9:18. When the positive class appears again, they are "the **objects of mercy**, which he has prepared beforehand for **glory**—including us whom he has **called**, not from the Jews only but also from the Gentiles," and they are named "**children of the living God**." (9:23–26)

The verb in 9:23 is worth stopping on. *Proētoimasen* — prepared beforehand. Paul does not say the objects of mercy turned out to be glorified. He says they were prepared, in advance, for glory. The antecedence is in the verb, and the terminus is in its complement, *for glory*. Both are Paul's.

Set the terms side by side:

children of God → children of the living God.
His call → us whom he has called.
Mercy → objects of mercy.

Three markers recur, and they are the markers by which Paul constituted the class in the first place. That answers the precedent-then-application reading. An opponent holding it must say Paul moved to a different population and described it with the same vocabulary he had used to define the one he left — divine sonship at both ends, calling at both ends, mercy at both ends, and no announcement of a change.

Two more things in the same stretch make the division harder still.

**Paul uses the word.** Immediately after naming them children of the living God, he quotes Isaiah: "Though the number of the children of Israel were like the sand of the sea, only a remnant of them **will be saved**." (9:27) That answers, in the same paragraph, the question he asked at 9:6. *Not all Israelites truly belong to Israel* is posed at the start; *only a remnant will be saved* is where it lands. The identity question and the salvation question are the same question for Paul.

**Paul marks his own conclusion.** At 9:30 he writes "What then are we to say?" — an inferential marker, drawing the result of what precedes. The result is soteriological: Gentiles who did not pursue righteousness attained it by faith, and Israel did not (9:30–33). So the election material is not left behind when salvation comes up. Paul says he is concluding from it.

So the class Paul constitutes through Isaac and Jacob has glory as its terminus, on his own account of it. The full chain: children of God (9:8) → purpose of election, his call (9:11–12) → mercy (9:16, 18) → objects of mercy prepared for glory (9:23) → us whom he has called (9:24) → children of the living God (9:26) → the remnant will be saved (9:27) → and therefore, who attained righteousness (9:30–33).

An opponent who divides this has to say Paul introduced the children of God, explained Isaac and Jacob by election and calling and mercy, described called objects of mercy prepared for glory, named them children of the living God, said the remnant will be saved, and then drew his conclusion about who attains righteousness — while having changed populations somewhere in the middle without saying so.

**R3. Paul places believers in that class.**

"For in Christ Jesus you are all **children of God** through faith... And if you belong to Christ, then you are **Abraham's offspring, heirs according to the promise**." (Gal 3:26, 29) "Now you, my friends, are **children of the promise, like Isaac**." (Gal 4:28)

Not a resemblance in one respect. Paul interlocks four terms across two chapters — children of God, Abraham's offspring, heirs according to the promise, children of the promise like Isaac — and they are the terms by which Romans 9 constitutes its class. Romans: children of God, children of the promise, election, calling, mercy, glory, children of the living God. Galatians: children of God, Abraham's offspring, heirs according to the promise, children of the promise like Isaac.

An opponent who says the phrase means one thing in Romans and another in Galatians has to say it of four terms at once, in the same writer, about the same patriarch.

**Two ways Paul picks out this class, and they do different work.** He recognizes its members by faith — those who belong to Christ are Abraham's offspring (Gal 3:29), and "those who believe are the descendants of Abraham" (Gal 3:7). He says what constitutes it by election — the purpose of election standing before either twin had done anything (Rom 9:11). Recognition and constitution are different questions, and Paul supplies an answer to each. Recognition does not make faith only a sign of membership. Paul says believers are children of God through faith (Gal 3:26), so faith is how a member comes to be in the class. The distinction concerns what the class is, not how one enters it. The labels are this argument's, not his — what the texts give directly is that believers are identified by faith and that the Isaac and Jacob discrimination is placed under the purpose of election.

This matters against a charge of circularity. If the class were simply *those who believe*, saying it is elected to salvation would restate that believers are saved. But Paul does not constitute the class by faith. He constitutes it by God's choosing and identifies its members by their faith. Which of the two explains the other is the question this paper brackets.

**So by this route as well:** the children of the promise are a class constituted by election whose terminus is glory, and Galatians places believers in it.
