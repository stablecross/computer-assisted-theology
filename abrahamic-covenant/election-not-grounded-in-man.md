*Companion to "Election in the Abrahamic Covenant Is to Salvation." That paper established the terminus and bracketed the basis. This one takes up the basis.*

**Claim under review.** The election of the promise-class, **as Scripture gives it**, is not conditioned on works, and it does not rest on the man's willing or running. No responsive fact about the man other than faith has textual warrant as the condition his election depends on. For faith, actual or foreseen, this paper shows that the reading with no human criterion is better supported than the reading that makes faith the criterion. It does not show here that the second reading is unavailable.

**The title claims more than this paper completes.** The title's claim is that no responsive fact of the man, faith included, grounds his election. This paper establishes it for every candidate except faith, and for faith it establishes the comparative result above. The synthesis paper of this series completes it, from the conclusions of "No One Comes to Christ Unless God First Changes the Heart" and "God Brings Every Heir to Faith and Loses None of Them."

Those three words are not a hedge and they are not decoration. They name the scope of the whole claim, and the evidential rule two paragraphs below says what they mean. A reader who drops them will hear a metaphysical assertion the paper does not make and no step below reaches.

**The class of facts the thesis is about.** Call a fact about a man **responsive** when what it states is his responding — that he does, that he will, or that he would under other circumstances. Faith is responsive, and this paper grants throughout that faith is the man's own act. Works are responsive. A decision is responsive, and so is what a man would have decided. Weakness, poverty, low birth, and being the younger of two twins are not responsive, because none of them states the man's responding at all.

The mode does not matter, and this paper does not ask what stands behind the response. A fact proposed as the condition of election is responsive when its content is the man's responding, whatever the proposer says that response consists in or arises from. So the classification never turns on a description of the will's state or structure — capacity, ability, inclination, bondage, freedom — and it is kept clear of those deliberately, because they belong to the question of total inability, which this paper brackets. A responsive fact is a fact about the man's responding whether or not he performs it, and whatever accounts for his performing it.

**Granted going in.** That the election is to salvation, which the companion paper establishes and this one imports. That faith is necessary, real, and the man's own act — which is not an import but a concession the argument runs against, since steps 4 and 5 need faith to be the man's act in order for foreseen faith to be a fact about him. Neither decides the question.

**Not under review.** How many are outside the class, and why. Step 6a argues that at least one man was passed over in the designation — Esau, whom Hebrews says was rejected when he sought to inherit the blessing — and that is all the extension this argument needs. It does not claim to know Esau's final state. It does not follow, and is not claimed, that any other named person was passed over, nor does this paper take up why anyone is passed over. Whether God's choosing may select on a non-responsive property of the man — something he is rather than something he does: this paper does not deny it and does not assert it, no step below tests such a candidate, and the ledger at step 2b runs on the responsive ones. Total inability, particular redemption, and perseverance are likewise elsewhere.

**What this argument does not import.** The companion paper imports no metaphysics, and neither does this one. The trilemma at step 1 does not assume that every difference has an account — fork three is precisely the position that this one has none, and it is answered from the text at step 2 rather than ruled out in advance. Nothing here depends on an account of how responsibility attaches to an act, on who owns an act, or on any claim about what would be fair. Those doors stay shut.

One narrow observation, and no more. Paul himself anticipates the objection this thesis invites — why then does he still find fault, for who can resist his will (9:19) — and what follows in 9:20–24 does not answer it by inserting a human differentiating condition back into 9:11–18. What those verses do answer, and how, is a question this paper does not take up.

**How the parts depend on each other.** Steps 1 through 7 are the elimination. They carry the claim as stated above: they exclude works and willing outright, and for faith they carry a comparative result, not an exclusion. Be precise about what carries what. The trilemma turns on 9:16's two clauses, and that part is deductive. The premises the trilemma stands on do not all rest there: step 2a restricts the field of candidates on textual grounds, and step 2b argues from a ledger of eight data across 9:11–19 and 11:7 that a human criterion can be placed beneath Paul's terms only at an unmarked cost. That second argument is comparative, and the conclusion says so. Step 7a is a third kind of argument and should be marked as one: it is not a reading of any passage but a pattern across all of them, and it is cumulative rather than deductive. It reaches the same conclusion by a route that does not depend on 2a's bridge or 2b's ledger, so it survives their loss — but on its own it establishes a pattern with no counterexample, not an exclusion. Steps 8 and 9 state an order: belonging and appointment standing before believing. Step 10 supplies something different — source evidence, that the new birth is not produced by human will but by God — without settling where the birth stands relative to faith. Those are independent of the elimination and survive its loss, but they do not by themselves exclude foreseen faith, since an objector can say God appointed, gave, and begot those he foreknew would believe. A reader who restricts 9:16 to the two patriarchs keeps the order-texts and loses the thesis as stated.

One dependency runs outside this paper and should be named where a reader will see it. Step 11 crosses from Romans 9's promise-class to believers, and the companion paper crosses the same gap. Both need one proposition: that believers belong to the Abrahamic promise-heir class. If it fails, this paper's thesis shrinks to two patriarchs and the companion's to three men in Genesis, so it is the premise an opponent should attack first in either.

Two supports reach it, and they fall to different attacks. Galatians 4:28 states it with Isaac named — children of the promise, like Isaac — and is attacked by reading *like* as resemblance. Galatians 3:29 states it without any comparison, and Hebrews 11:9 puts Isaac on the same side of that description as heir of the same promise. The thin-*like* attack has nothing to grip on the second. An opponent must therefore break the proposition twice, by different arguments.

Step 6a is a special case and should be labeled as one. It argues that at least one man was passed over in the designation, which keeps the two-man test from having an empty domain. It is not load-bearing: 2a's per-instance reading of 9:16 covers the same ground by another route, and it was added for that purpose. So an objector who defeats 6a has removed a case from the test and has not touched the thesis.

**What "election" names in this paper.** The antecedent divine determination by which a person is assigned the salvific destiny of the promise-class. Romans 9 calls the governing purpose *election*; Romans 8 describes the same persons as foreknown, predestined, called, justified, and glorified. The vocabulary differs and the object is one: an act of God, with a person as its object, whose terminus is that person's salvation.

The definition is stated because a label dispute would otherwise substitute for the question. An objector who says *I do not call that election* has not answered anything; he still has to say whether that determination is contingent on a responsive human qualifying condition, and that is what the thesis denies.

**Which question is under review.** Not whether God ordained that there would be a promise-class. The question is what grounds that determination for a given person — and, where two men differ, what explains the discriminatory mercy Paul describes. A reader may hold that God unconditionally chose the class "those in Christ" while personal membership in it is conditioned on faith. Before that is classified, notice that *election is corporate* does not by itself say anything about the question under review. Three models go by the name, and they answer differently.

**Aggregate.** God chooses persons; the corporation is the set those choices constitute. The collective term describes the result. Personal election is doing the work, and the trilemma partitions its ground directly.

**Corporate with personal election.** God chooses the body and also chooses its members. The corporate dimension is real and does not compete with personal election; the only question left is what grounds the latter, which is the question this paper asks.

**Corporate-first.** God chooses the body without yet choosing which persons will constitute it, and a membership rule — faith — determines who is in. This is the only one of the three that is distinctively corporate, and it is the position the rest of this paragraph and step 11 are about.

So the sorting question is not corporate versus individual. It is whether God's choice determines the roster or some further condition does. On the first two models the answer is God's choice, and the objector has conceded the object of the thesis while disputing its ground. On the third the answer is faith.

And the third comes in two forms. If the advocate identifies personal membership with personal election — God elects a man by bringing him into the body, and faith is what brings him — then faith is the condition his election depends on, and that is fork two. If he denies any antecedent personal determination of a person's salvific destiny, he holds the stronger form, which is not fork two but a denial that the question has an object. Step 11 answers that one.

Ephesians 1:4 is where that reader will stand: God "chose us **in Christ** before the foundation of the world." The phrase does bear a corporate reading, and this paper does not dispute that God's choosing is in Christ. The corporate reading of *in him* settles where the choosing is located, not what differentiates its objects.

**The stronger form of that position, and the answer to it.** A careful advocate will not say the man's faith grounds his election. He will say there is no individual election to be grounded: God chose the body, persons join it by faith, and no person was chosen rather than another. That is not fork two. It denies that the question has an object.

Paul's own grammar supplies the object. "I will have mercy on **whom** I have mercy" (9:15). "He has mercy on **whomever** he chooses, and he hardens the heart of **whomever** he chooses" (9:18). Pharaoh, by name (9:17). "Including us **whom** he has called" (9:24). And the cases the whole argument is drawn from are two men, one preferred to the other, before either had done anything. Whatever corporate structure the class has, the mercy and the calling of Romans 9 have human objects distributed among them. So the question stands: what explains why a given person is among them. Once the personal objects are granted, the careful advocate has one escape left: to hold that the personal data establish mercy and calling on persons without establishing an antecedent personal election, so that election is corporate and faith conditions participation in it. That is a real position with published defenders, and it does not need a seam — it lets election, calling, and mercy all run through the chapter as three relations rather than one. Step 11 answers it, and not from vocabulary continuity: Romans 8:29–30 predicates an antecedent predestining of persons — *hous*, not a body — and runs the same objects to a personal justifying and glorifying. That is the determination this paper's thesis is about, whatever it is called, and the corporate advocate must then say what conditions a person's being among its objects.

Ephesians 1:13 is the corporate reader's best further text — "in him you also, when you had heard the word of truth... and had believed in him, were marked with the seal of the promised Holy Spirit." Sealing follows believing; the verse says so. What it does not say is that the choosing of 1:4 followed anything. It tells you when a man is sealed, not what grounds his being chosen.

**What "grounded in" means here.** A fact in the man grounds the election when a person's being elected is contingent on his satisfying it — when the fact functions as a qualifying condition, so that a person who did not satisfy it would not be elected.

Note what the definition does *not* require. It does not require that some actual person fail to satisfy the condition. A requirement does not stop being a requirement because everyone happens to meet it. Suppose God elects on condition of faith and every human being eventually believes: faith is still the condition his election depends on, and the thesis still denies it. The contrast between two men is how the paper *tests* for a condition, not what makes something one. Keeping those apart matters twice over: it is why a universally met condition still falls under fork two, and it is why 2a's bridge is given a per-instance reading alongside its contrastive one.

Note also what the definition does *not* by itself settle. It states the grounding relation, and it states it over facts in the man generally. It does not state the range the thesis quantifies over. That range is the responsive facts defined above, and the two must be kept apart: a non-responsive property may stand in the grounding relation for all this paper argues, and the thesis is silent about it.

Two consequences, and the definition is stated rather than assumed because both are doing work.

A fact may be non-meritorious and still be grounding. If two men receive identical grace and God elects the one whose foreseen faith satisfies a condition, that man's faith is the qualifying condition, and calling it a condition rather than a ground does not change what it does. Merit is a separate question and is taken up at step 5.

And a property of the chosen is not thereby a ground of the kind the thesis denies. God may choose objects of a certain kind because that kind suits a purpose he formed independently. Whether the individual's possession of such a property qualifies him is a question about a non-responsive property, which this paper leaves open; what the thesis denies is contingency on a responsive human fact. It does not deny that the chosen have properties, or that their properties serve God's purposes. Step 7b works through the case that makes this distinction necessary.

**The evidential rule this paper runs on, and the scope it gives the claim.** A proposed factor may not function as a premise in reading a text merely because the text leaves it logically possible. If the factor does explanatory work in a reading, that reading must show the corpus states it, entails it, or warrants it elsewhere. A factor with no such warrant is not thereby declared impossible. It is unrevealed, and therefore unavailable as an account of what the text explains.

The thesis is stated at that scope. It says that election, as Scripture gives it, is not conditioned on a responsive human fact. For faith it says this at the comparative strength stated above. It does not say that no such condition could exist in any description of reality, and no argument below reaches that. A reader who wants the second claim will not find it here; a reader who thinks the first is the weaker of the two has mistaken which question the paper asks.

**A burden-shift the objector will try, and why it fails.** Told that his criterion has no warrant, he answers: if nothing forbids it, then something had better deny it. Produce a verse saying God uses no criterion.

Three things about that demand.

It inverts the burden. On it, every proposition the corpus does not expressly refute becomes available as a premise — foreseen humility, foreseen non-resistance, what a man would do in some other circumstance, any of them. Nothing denies any of them. A corpus that licenses whatever it does not deny licenses everything.

It is unsatisfiable in principle, which is what makes it a demand rather than a test. Scripture nowhere denies the existence of unstated explanatory levels, about this or anything else. A rule requiring explicit denial would leave every reading of every passage permanently open.

And it is answered. Paul does deny. *Not of the one willing, nor of the one running* (Rom 9:16, literally) is a denial, and the clause after it names what the matter does rest on. So the question was never whether a denial exists. It is how far that denial reaches — which is the extension question, argued evidentially at step 2a and comparatively at step 2b.

**What this paper adds that the text does not mark, stated here rather than left for an objector.** Both readings supply something. This one distinguishes faith as the means of attaining righteousness from election as the ground of the called objects. That distinction has positive markers: 9:30–32 ties faith to attaining, 9:11–18 uses election, calling, mercy, and God's will for the prior question, and 11:7 names the successful side by election. The criterion model supplies a relation — election contingent on the man's faith — that no sentence marks. The comparison at 2b is between those two additions, not between an addition and none.

**What an objection has to do.** Show one of three things. That Romans 9:16 is not answering the contrastive question this paper assigns to it — that the verse states where mercy comes from without saying why it falls on one man and not another. Or that naming God as the source leaves room for God to apply a human criterion, so that source-exclusivity does not reach criterion-exclusivity. Or that a human response can be the differentiating condition without becoming part of what Paul says the matter does not rest on. Step 2a argues the first, step 2b the second, steps 4 and 5 the third. Showing any of these would be a failure of the offered proof. The objector does not have to prove that election is conditioned. Saying the difference is the man's free response is not by itself any of the three; it is the question restated with a word in front of it.

## The question

Two men. Same nature. Same gospel preached. Same conviction by the Spirit, the same drawing, the same grace — grant the objector everything he wants on that score, and make it as identical as he likes; step 5 asks what follows if it is not. One believes. One does not.

Two things granted at the front turn that scene into the question this paper asks. Election is to salvation, and faith is the means. Add the thing every party to this dispute already holds — that salvation is by God's mercy — and where the mercy has fallen, a man believes.

State the scene's limits, because it is an illustration and not a verdict. That B does not believe today does not tell you B is outside the class; he may believe tomorrow. Step 6a names one man Scripture says was passed over in the designation, and no other individual is claimed. The two men are a way of putting the question in front of you, not a ruling on either man. What the illustration displays is a difference in outcome under the same conditions, and the question is what accounts for such a difference wherever it obtains.

So the question is not, in the first instance, what produced one man's believing. It is what accounts for the mercy falling where it falls — the discriminatory election the front matter named. That is what Romans 9:16 speaks to, and that is the difference the forks below are drawn about.

What accounts for the difference?

## The trilemma

**1. A person's election has a ground that includes no responsive qualifying condition, or a ground that includes one, or no ground at all.**

State the forks carefully, and state what they partition, because both are places a loose version leaks.

**What they partition.** The ground of *a person's election* as the front matter defines it — the antecedent divine determination assigning that person the salvific destiny of the promise-class. Not the ground of the difference between two men. The two-man scene is how the paper tests for a condition, since a condition shows itself where one man meets it and another does not. It is not the explanandum the forks divide. Cutting them on the A/B difference would leave a condition everyone satisfies with nowhere to sit, and such a condition is precisely what fork two is written to catch.

**The predicate they are cut on** is the thesis's own: whether a responsive human qualifying condition is part of that ground.

*Fork one.* Something grounds a person's election, and whatever it includes, it includes no responsive qualifying condition belonging to the man.

*Fork two.* The ground includes, wholly or partly, a responsive qualifying condition on which a person's election is contingent — whether or not some other actual person fails to satisfy it. A joint account, where God acts and the man's response is also necessary, belongs here. So does agent causation. So does a condition every man in fact meets. The fork is not "the man alone"; it is "the man's act at all."

*Fork three.* Nothing grounds it. A person is elected and no explanation accounts for that. Note the object: it is the election, not the man's believing. A brute account of the believing is a different claim and is taken up at step 2.

These are exhaustive. Either something grounds a person's election or nothing does — fork three against the other two. If something does, the ground either includes a responsive human qualifying condition or it does not — fork two against fork one.

Note what fork one takes in. Any ground that is not a responsive human qualifying condition: a divine act, a circumstance, another creature, an impersonal cause — and also a non-responsive property of the man, which is the case step 7b works through. Nothing here rules any of those out, and nothing needs to. Whatever fork one contains, it does not contain what the thesis denies.

Two narrower cuts would leave gaps, and both are tempting. Cutting fork one as "God's act" leaves a ground that is neither the man's nor God's with nowhere to sit, since a ground of that kind is not thereby no ground at all. Cutting it as "not in the man" leaves a non-responsive property of the man with nowhere to sit either, and this paper does not claim to have excluded one. The predicate above avoids both.

The narrowed predicate also fits the verse the elimination turns on. Romans 9:16 excludes *ho thelōn* and *ho trechōn* — the one willing and the one running. Willing is an act of the will and running is effort, so both are responsive, and the fork is cut on the class Paul's own two terms belong to.

And one position sits outside all three. Step 6 takes it up: that there is no personal election to be grounded at all. That is not a fork — it denies that the forks have an object, and step 11 is where the paper answers it.

What narrows fork one is the text rather than the logic. Romans 9:16 has two clauses. The first excludes fork two for willing and running, and step 2b weighs faith against it. The second does not merely deny — it names: *but of God who shows mercy*. That is what identifies the ground inside fork one instead of leaving it unspecified.

**2. Fork three: from neither.**

State the fork on the explanandum the trilemma partitions. A man is elected, and nothing whatever accounts for that — not him, not a circumstance, and not God. The election occurs and has no ground. Where two men differ, the same holds of the discrimination between them, since on this fork neither election has a ground.

Do not confuse this with a brute account of the *believing*. Someone may hold that A's faith arises with nothing accounting for it, and that is a different claim which leaves the election question open: if God elects those who in fact believe, the brute faith is the condition and the position is fork two; if God elects A on no condition at all, the brute faith says nothing about the election and fork one stands. Uncaused believing does not put anyone on fork three. What puts a position on fork three is holding that the election itself has no ground.

A reader should know that unexplained events are not an incoherent category, and the paper concedes it rather than disputing it. Events without prior sufficient cause appear to be real. Mermin's question about whether the moon is there when no one looks is the popular entry to the problem, and Conway and Kochen's Free Will Theorem is the sharper one: on their axioms, if the experimenter's choice is free in a specified sense, the particle's response is not a function of prior information either.[^1] The literature disputing what those results show is large, and this paper takes no position in it.

That material is an analogy and not an instance. It shows that *some event has no explanation* is a coherent thing to say. It does not supply a case of a discriminatory divine mercy without a ground, and no physics could. What it buys the objector is the right to state fork three without being accused of nonsense.

Grant him that. The category exists and does not fit the case.

"So it depends not on human will or exertion, **but on God who shows mercy**." (Rom 9:16)

The Greek is ἄρα οὖν οὐ τοῦ θέλοντος οὐδὲ τοῦ τρέχοντος ἀλλὰ τοῦ ἐλεῶντος θεοῦ — literally, *so then, not of the one willing, nor of the one running, but of the God who shows mercy*. Where the argument turns on the two participles, this paper uses that literal rendering, in italics.

The sentence has two clauses. It denies a ground and then names one.

Be careful about which thing fork three contradicts. It does **not** entail that no mercy was shown. A man believes; mercy may be shown to him however his believing arose. The same is true on fork two: the man contributes nothing meritorious, and mercy is still shown. So an argument of the form "on your view nobody was merciful to him" fails against both forks, and the paper does not make it.

What Paul's second clause states is a *dependence*. Not that mercy occurred, but that the matter **rests on** God who shows mercy. Fork three says it rests on nothing — not on the man, and not on God either. That denies the affirmation as directly as fork two denies the negation. Paul does not leave the question open at the point where fork three leaves it open; he says what the matter depends on, and fork three says it depends on nothing at all.

How the physics resolves, or whether it resolves, does not bear on this. The analogy was only ever establishing that unexplained events are sayable. Whether they are actual, the mercy of Romans 9 is said by Paul to rest on God, and a position that says it rests on nothing denies that clause. Either way the second clause stands where Paul put it.

**2a. The bridge this argument turns on.**

State the central move plainly, because everything after it depends on the move being sound.

"Grounded in," as defined above: a fact in the man grounds the election when a person's being elected is contingent on his satisfying it. Not merely that the fact is relevant to the choosing — that it is the condition the choosing turns on. Where two men differ, the contrast is how that condition shows itself, and 9:16 is read here as answering the contrast. Romans 9:16 is a statement of source. The Greek is οὐ τοῦ θέλοντος οὐδὲ τοῦ τρέχοντος ἀλλὰ τοῦ ἐλεῶντος θεοῦ — bare genitives with an implied verb: it is not of the one willing, nor of the one running, but of the mercy-showing God.

There is no noun for the *it*, so what fills the gap has to come from the paragraph. An objector will try to fill it narrowly — that what does not depend on human willing is the fulfillment of God's purpose, leaving open who its objects are. The gap is filled by what 9:11–15 has been about: God's purpose of election, and the mercy that purpose assigns. The companion paper establishes that this election is to salvation. So the *it* is not the abstract existence of a promised people; it is the assigning of saving mercy, and a narrower filling has to say what else 9:11–15 supplied for the sentence to be about. The bare genitive construction is naturally read as expressing source or dependence. Five verses earlier Paul says the same thing with the preposition spelled out: the purpose stands *οὐκ ἐξ ἔργων ἀλλ' ἐκ τοῦ καλοῦντος* — not out of works but out of the one who calls (9:11–12). Different constructions do not become identical by proximity, but the source-reading of 9:16 is strongly supported by 9:11's explicit ἐκ, since Paul is drawing the same divine-side against human-side line in both. Source and contrast are not the same relation. So an objector can say Paul excludes human willing as the source of mercy — grace is the source, nobody disputes that — while holding that the man's response is what contrasts him with his neighbor.

The bridge is that Paul's source-statement is his answer to a contrast-question. Three things in the immediate context establish it.

The verse opens with *ara oun* — so then — concluding an argument that runs from the two brothers through the injustice question. The immediate antecedent is 9:15, which answers 9:14, which arises from 9:11–13. The mediation strengthens the reading rather than weakening it: the charge at 9:14 is that God is unjust, and what provoked it was the contrast between Jacob and Esau. So the conclusion at 9:16 is drawn from a chain whose subject throughout is one brother preferred to the other.

Verse 15 and verse 18 are distributive: mercy on **whom** I have mercy; mercy on whomever he chooses. The NRSV's *chooses* renders θέλει, *he wills*, the verb of 9:16's *the one willing*.

And verse 18 sets mercy against hardening in one sentence, which is a contrast or it is nothing.

**The bridge also runs a second way, and the thesis needs both.** Read contrastively, 9:16 says what accounts for mercy falling on one man and not another. Read as it stands, it says what the matter rests on, full stop: not of the one willing, nor of the one running, but of God who shows mercy. That second reading is about each instance rather than about the difference between two.

Why the paper needs it. Fork two takes in a responsive condition every man in fact meets. A condition of that kind shows up in the contrast test only when some man fails it. So if the whole bridge were contrastive, a universally met condition would sit in fork two untouched by the argument that empties fork two.

The per-instance reading closes that, and it is worth being exact about how, because a stronger claim is available here and does not hold. It does not establish on its own that naming God as the source excludes a criterion beneath him. That inference fails — a university admission rests on the university and is still conditioned on a grade — and step 2b exists because it fails.

What the per-instance reading does is fix where 2b's conclusion attaches. Step 2b establishes that no responsive criterion is marked in this passage, and that the reading with none is better supported than the reading with faith. Read per-instance, 9:16 says that result holds of each man's election rather than only of the difference between two men. A condition every man meets is therefore reached by 2b, and the per-instance reading is what carries 2b to it. A condition with no textual warrant is excluded. Faith is ranked below its rival, not excluded.

Two of 2b's planks are already non-contrastive in the same way: 9:11–12 names the Caller rather than a rule the called satisfy, and 9:15's *idem per idem* withholds the specification without reference to any contrast.

So the contrastive reading and the per-instance reading do different work, and neither is decorative. Step 6a argues separately that the contrast case is not empty — that some man was in fact passed over in the designation — but the coverage above does not depend on that step.

An objector will press the parallel. Exodus says Pharaoh hardened his own heart and also says God hardened it, so human facts are evidently not excluded from the hardening side; why should they be excluded from the mercy side? Note that the premise comes from Exodus, not from 9:18, and the answer has to meet it there.

Grant Exodus entirely. Pharaoh hardened himself; God hardened him; the paper takes no position on how the two relate. What 9:18 does is put both sides in one construction with the stated willing in God: *he has mercy on whom he wills, and he hardens whom he wills*. So whatever is true of Pharaoh in Exodus, Paul's sentence does not make either side turn on a condition the man meets — and the parallel the objector is pressing therefore runs the other way, since the symmetry he wants is a symmetry of God's willing. That is as far as the argument goes. Whether Pharaoh's own hardening stands causally before, after, or in some other relation to God's hardening is not decided here and need not be. What the paper does not do is answer why God hardens whom he hardens. That is bracketed at the front, and Paul raises it himself at 9:19.

**Why the named human terms are enough for the countermodels actually in evidence.** An objector will press the extension. Paul denies two things — the one willing, the one running. The thesis denies responsive qualifying conditions beyond those two. Those are not obviously the same negation, and the paper should not pretend they are by fiat. A parallel makes the worry concrete: *it rests not on the applicant who desires or strives, but on the university that admits* is true of a university with a grade requirement, and the requirement is still a qualifying condition.

The answer is evidentiary, and the paper should say so rather than claim more. It does not claim that *willing* and *running* lexically enumerate every responsive act a man might perform, and it does not claim that every human act other than faith is a work. That second claim would be false on this paper's own terms, since faith is granted to be the man's act and is not a work in Paul's sense.

What the texts do establish is narrower and sufficient.

**Works are excluded from the basis of choosing, by name.** The purpose stands *not out of works but out of the one who calls* (Rom 9:11–12). And where the subject is a *chosen* remnant: "if it is by grace, it is no longer on the basis of works; otherwise grace would no longer be grace" (Rom 11:6).

**Faith is the human-side candidate the dispute actually turns on, and Paul marks it as distinct from works.** "Now to one who works, wages are not reckoned as a gift but as something due. But to one who without works trusts him who justifies the ungodly, such faith is reckoned as righteousness." (Rom 4:4–5) So faith cannot be dismissed as a work, and it has to be tested on its own. The same opposition recurs wherever Paul raises what a man brings: not by works of the law but through faith (Gal 2:16); not a law of works but a law of faith (Rom 3:27); not a righteousness of my own from the law but the one through faith (Phil 3:9); and in the argument under review, Israel fails because it pursued not by faith but as if by works (Rom 9:32).

So the two candidates with positive textual standing are works and faith. Works fall to 9:11 and 11:6. Faith is tested at 2b, 4, and 5.

**On a third candidate.** An objector may propose repentance, humility, fear, non-resistance, or something else. This paper does not answer by classifying those as works — that inference does not hold, and faith is the counterexample to it. It answers by the standard the method already sets: a countermodel needs a positively warranted textual candidate, not a conceivable one. The objector must produce a text making his candidate the condition on which a person's election is contingent — not the means of receiving, not the fruit of election, not a description of the elect. If he produces one, and the candidate is responsive, step 2b's generic test applies to it as it applies to faith, and this argument has to answer it. A candidate that is not responsive falls outside what the thesis denies, and the front matter has already said so.

**One candidate is usually produced here, and it should be answered rather than left to the challenge.** "God shows no partiality, but in every nation anyone who **fears him and does what is right** is acceptable to him." (Acts 10:34–35) A human quality, named as what makes a man acceptable, spoken by Peter at the moment God's reach extends to a Gentile.

Read what *dektos* is predicated of in the episode. The angel tells Cornelius that his prayers and alms "have ascended as a memorial before God" (10:4, 10:31); the question the vision answers is whether God regards a Gentile's devotion at all, and Peter's *no partiality* is an answer about nations, not about a qualification for election. And Cornelius is not yet where the passage leaves him: Peter is to speak "a message by which you and your entire household will be saved" (11:14). So the verse denies that God's regard is bounded by nation. It does not state a condition on which anyone's election depends.

What the pairing of willing and running does contribute is to block the narrow reading — that only meritorious achievement is excluded — since running is effort and willing is the act of deciding, and neither is a wage.

So the question Paul is answering when he writes *not of the one willing* is why one and not the other. His answer to that question is a source-statement, and the source he names is not the man. An objector who wants to keep the source-reading while denying the contrast-reading has to say what question 9:16 is the *ara oun* of.

**2b. From source to criterion.**

One more step is needed, and it is the one an objector will hold out on longest. Grant that 9:16 answers the contrast question and names God as the source. He replies: God being the source does not exclude God having a subordinate human criterion, call it *C*. Two answers can be true at different levels — ultimately God, proximately the man's satisfying *C* — and Paul gave the first without denying the second. Faith is the live proposal in this dispute, but the bridge has to address the generic form if the thesis is to hold across the responsive range.

The parallel he will use is fair and should be stated. *It depends not on the applicant who desires or strives, but on the university that admits* is true of a university with a grade requirement, and the requirement is still a qualifying condition. So naming the decider does not by itself exclude a rule the decider applies.

The question here is therefore comparative, not merely logical. No single silence proves that no criterion exists. The criterion model survives only if it can explain the positive shape of the passage at comparable cost. Put the relevant data on the ledger.

**First: 9:11–12 names the caller, not a rule the called satisfy.** The purpose of election stands *not out of works but out of the one who calls*. A criterion model can say the Caller is the ultimate source while faith is the subordinate rule. That is coherent, but it adds a second explanatory level that Paul does not mark in the sentence. On the reading defended here, the positive term Paul names is the explanatory term the argument needs.

**Second: 9:15 states no criterion.** Paul's premise is Exodus 33:19, in the form he quotes it: "I will have mercy on whom I have mercy, and I will have compassion on whom I have compassion" (Rom 9:15). That is the *idem per idem* formula — a Hebrew construction in which the verb is repeated with an indefinite relative, as in "I AM WHO I AM" (Exod 3:14) and, literally, *as I am bereaved, I am bereaved* (Gen 43:14; the NRSV has "if I am bereaved of my children, I am bereaved").

Its force is disputed and the dispute should be named. On one reading the construction is indefinite: it asserts the action while declining to state what determines its object. On another it is intensive — *I will surely have mercy on whom I choose* — expressing the freedom of the agent rather than the absence of a rule. The second reading is compatible with a criterion the agent freely applies, and an objector may take it.

Two things favor the indefinite reading, and one argument that suggests itself must be set aside. The nearest parallel is Exodus 3:14, which is not naturally an intensive assertion that God surely is. And the construction is described in the grammatical literature as a form used where greater explicitness is withheld — Driver's account, followed by Lundbom; the NET translators likewise note that in Exodus 33:19 the verbs state what God will do without explaining why.[^2]

Set aside this argument: that only the indefinite reading yields 9:16's negation. It does not hold. *God freely determines whom to have mercy on* can support *not of the one willing, nor of the one running*. What the intensive reading does not itself establish is the further claim that no subordinate criterion is in play — and that further claim is what this section is testing, so it cannot also be the premise that selects the reading.

State the plank's weight accordingly. On the indefinite reading, the formula states no criterion. That is all it shows. Non-specification is not exclusion, and it does not count against a criterion supplied from context. Driver and the NET note both read the recipients from the surrounding episode, where Moses has interceded and God says, "you have found favor in my sight, and I know you by name" (Exod 33:17). So this plank shows only that the formula is silent, and the comparison below does not count that silence as a cost.

**Third: 9:16 is the *ara oun* of that premise and states dependence.** Verse 15 has already asserted God's mercy. Verse 16 adds the explicit contrast: not of the one willing or running, but of the mercy-showing God. On a criterion model this conclusion must operate only at the higher level — source — while the lower-level selection still turns on the man's satisfying *C* (faith in the standard version). That is possible, but the two-level source/criterion distinction is supplied by the model rather than marked by the sentence.

**Fourth: 9:17 gives Pharaoh as the next person-case and explains his place by God's purpose.** "I have raised you up for the very purpose of showing my power in you" does not by itself settle the ground of mercy, and this paper does not use Pharaoh to answer why God hardens. Its narrower evidentiary force is directional: when Paul moves to another named individual, the explanation he states is again on God's side rather than in a qualification Pharaoh satisfied.

**Fifth: 9:18 makes God's will distributive twice.** *He has mercy on whom he wills, and he hardens whom he wills.* A criterion model can read the divine will at a higher level: God wills a rule — mercy to those satisfying *C*, hardening according to whatever rule the model assigns the other side — and the creature's condition determines who satisfies the rule. But then *whom he wills* is not the immediate differentiator of the objects; satisfaction of the unstated criterion is. The model therefore needs the same unmarked two-level distinction in a sentence whose grammatical subject and repeated *whom he wills* place the stated willing in God. The point does not depend on making the mercy and hardening criteria symmetrical.

**Sixth: 9:19 is corroborative only.** *Why then does he still find fault? For who can resist his will?* This verse does not prove that the objector read 9:16 as excluding every criterion. The hardening clause in 9:18 can motivate the fault-finding objection whatever one thinks about the mercy side. So the paper does not use 9:19 as a load-bearing inference about what the objector must have understood. The narrower observation is simply that Paul answers the objection without restoring a human criterion. That absence fits this reading, but because the objection can arise from hardening it carries less weight than the data above.

**Seventh: when Paul restates the division, he names the successful group by election.** *Israel failed to obtain what it was seeking. The elect obtained it, but the rest were hardened* (Rom 11:7). A criterion model can read *the elect* as those whom God elected according to foreseen faith, and that is coherent — *elect* is a fine label for whomever God elected, by whatever means.

So do not charge the model with inventing a category. Charge it with the word Paul chose. He is restating the very contrast of 9:30–32, where faith was named; and restating it, he writes *the elect obtained it*, not *the believers obtained it*. The second sentence was available and says what the criterion model would have him mean.

**Eighth: the objection Paul raises at 9:14 is the one a criterion model answers on the spot.** "What then are we to say? Is there injustice on God's part? By no means!" (9:14)

Ask what provokes it. A charge of injustice is provoked by a discrimination that appears to rest on nothing in the men. Suppose God chose Jacob because he foresaw what Jacob would be. Then the discrimination rests on exactly the kind of fact the objector's sense of justice is asking for, and the reply writes itself: God did not choose arbitrarily, he chose in view of what each man would be. A foreseen human criterion would supply a natural answer to the charge, and Paul does not supply it.

And note what Paul does with the objection. He does not resolve it by supplying the criterion. He quotes Exodus — *I will have mercy on whom I have mercy* — and concludes *not of the one willing, nor of the one running*. He answers a charge of injustice by restating the thing that provoked it.

The objection is also Paul's own construction, and that bears on what may be read into it. He controls the sequence. He states that the word came before the twins were born and before either had done anything; he subordinates that circumstance to a purpose clause, so that the purpose of election might stand, not by works but by the Caller; and then he raises the injustice charge himself. A reader who posits an unstated foreseen criterion operating behind that sequence is positing a premise that would have dissolved Paul's own objection, and Paul's actual answer is evidence about how he takes his preceding argument to run.

This is a stronger form of the sixth plank above, and the difference is worth marking. At 9:19 the objection is *why does he still find fault*, and the hardening clause at 9:18 motivates that question on any reading of the mercy clause, so the plank had to be narrowed. At 9:14 hardening is not yet in view, so that motivation is absent. The charge still has more than one possible source. It can arise from the chapter's frame, the apparent failure of the promise among Paul's kin (9:6), or from the pre-birth timing alone. The paragraph below takes up the second. What this plank uses is what Paul does with the charge: he answers it without supplying a criterion.

A criterion advocate can reply that the injustice charge arises from the pre-birth timing by itself — a reader told that God announced before either had acted may call it arbitrary without yet having been told about foreseen faith. That is available, and it costs him a resolvable objection left unresolved through 9:15–18 and answered at 9:20 by refusing the questioner's standing. It also requires him to supply the hidden criterion rather than locate it, and the plank is cumulative rather than the hinge, so the paper presses it no further.

**Where Scripture does ground God's act in a man's response, it says so.** The distinction the criterion model needs — God the source, a human response the condition beneath him — is not foreign to Scripture. Judgment is the clearest case. Deception works on "those who are perishing, because they refused to love the truth and so be saved. For this reason God sends them a powerful delusion" (2 Thess 2:10–11). Recompense is another: "he will repay according to each one's deeds" (Rom 2:6). So the two-level distinction is not an invented level, and this paper does not treat it as one.

What those texts show is how Scripture writes the relation when it holds. It names the response and marks it as the reason: *because*, *for this reason*, *according to*. At election the markers run the other way: *not by works but by his call* (9:11–12), *not of the one willing, nor of the one running* (9:16). So the cost charged below is not that the distinction is unknown. It is that the corpus marks the distinction where it applies, and does not mark it here.

Now compare costs rather than asking whether a criterion model is logically possible. It can accommodate every datum, and it does so by one governing distinction rather than several: God determines the gracious rule, and the man's satisfying *C* determines who falls under it.

An objector will say that is a single posit, not five epicycles, and he is right about the count. The count is not the point. The cost is that the distinction is unmarked at every place it has to be applied. Paul names the Caller at 9:11–12 and does not name a rule the called meet. He states the dependence at 9:16 with no second level in view. He places the willing in God twice at 9:18. He calls the successful side *the elect* at 11:7 where *the believers* was available. At each of those the model must supply a level the sentence does not mark — the same level each time, which is what makes it one distinction, and unmarked each time, which is what makes it a cost.

The standard foreseen-faith model simply sets *C = faith*.

**What the remaining disagreement actually is.** Two readings can share almost everything and still divide, and it is worth saying where the division falls, because a reader who misses it will answer this argument with texts it already grants.

Both readings may affirm that salvation is by grace; that faith is necessary, real, and the man's own act; that righteousness is received through faith; that believers are in Christ; that God calls, sanctifies, and saves. This paper grants every one of those, and steps 5a, 8 through 10, and the willing-texts section grant them in the texts an objector would cite for them.

The division is in the connection. On the criterion model God's provision comes first in every respect except one: the fact that distinguishes the object of election from the man beside him is the man's faith, actual or foreseen, so God's discriminating determination is responsive to a human-side fact. On the reading defended here the discriminating term is itself in God's purpose, calling, willing, and mercy, and the man's saving participation lies downstream of it.

State that carefully, because a loose version is a slogan and answerable as one. The claim is not that one reading has God acting first and the other has man acting first. On the criterion model God acts first in supplying the gospel, the grace, and the drawing. The claim is narrower and concerns one node: at the point where the election of this man rather than that one is explained, which side supplies the differentiating term. And the claim is a description of what the two readings do, arrived at from the texts above — not a principle brought to them. Romans 9:11–18 repeatedly puts that term on God's side; the criterion model must place a human one beneath it.

The reading defended here also makes a distinction — faith is the means by which righteousness is attained, not the ground of the election that supplies the called objects. But that distinction has positive textual markers: Romans 9:30–32 explicitly names faith in connection with attaining righteousness, while 9:11–18 names election, calling, mercy, and God's will in the explanation of the personal contrast, and 11:7 restates the successful side as *the elect*. Under the comparative test, the criterion model is possible but explanatorily more expensive and less directly marked by the text.

That is the bridge from source to criterion. It is not the invalid inference *God is source, therefore no criterion*. It is the cumulative judgment that any proposed responsive election-criterion *C* has to be inserted beneath the positive explanatory terms Paul actually supplies. A positively marked third criterion could reopen the question; absent such a marker, the generic criterion model is not an equally supported reading merely because it is logically consistent.

**State the result at its strength, and separately for faith.** For a responsive candidate with no text behind it, the evidential rule makes the criterion unavailable. For faith it does not, because an objector can assemble a case from texts that mark related relations: sonship through faith (Gal 3:26), choosing in Christ (Eph 1:4), and choosing through belief on the attachment step 5a grants (2 Thess 2:13). None of them states that election is contingent on faith. Ephesians 1:4 places the choosing "before the foundation of the world," so a union in Christ by faith can enter that choosing only as a union foreseen, and the case reduces to foreseen faith, which is step 4's subject. The case still has to be weighed rather than dismissed. It is weighed here and at step 5a, and the result is that the reading with no criterion is better supported. The step from better supported to excluded is not made in this paper. The synthesis makes it.

**3. Fork two: from the man.**

If a responsive qualifying condition belonging to the man wholly or partly grounds his election, the matter depends in that respect on the man. Paul's first clause denies two things by name — the one willing and the one running — and the route from those two words to the paper's thesis is not a single lexical step. Step 2a identifies the candidates with textual standing and excludes works, and step 2b tests faith held as God's criterion under the source/criterion distinction. What this step adds is what the pairing itself contributes: the exclusion reaches beyond meritorious achievement to willing as willing.

And 9:16 is not a fresh assertion at this point. Paul has already located the same thing five verses earlier: "Even before they had been born or had done anything good or bad (so that God's purpose of election might continue, **not by works but by his call**) she was told, 'The elder shall serve the younger.'" (Rom 9:11–12) The purpose stands by the call, not by what the person does.

Note what is denied. Willing is paired with running. Running is effort; willing is the act of the will as such. So the exclusion is not of merit only. An objector who says the man contributes nothing meritorious, only a decision, has not escaped the pairing.

**4. Foreseen faith is still a term on the man's side.**

The standard escape is that God elects on the basis of faith he foresees. Two things about it, and neither requires faith to fall under *thelein*.

**Timing is not ground.** Foreknowledge changes when God knows the man's faith. It does not change what the election is contingent on. If God elects A because A would believe, the *because* names a fact about A, and moving God's knowledge of it earlier leaves the fact where it was.

**And Paul says why the timing mattered to him, which is a claim about ground.** He does not merely report that the twins had done nothing. He subordinates that circumstance to a purpose clause: "Even before they had been born or had done anything good or bad (**so that** God's purpose of election might continue, not by works but by his call)" (9:11–12). The *hina* tells us what the pre-birth circumstance is there to secure — that the purpose stands on the Caller and not on human performance.

Now put a foreseen condition under it. God chooses Jacob because he foresees what Jacob will be. Then Paul's appeal loses most of its force: the twins' not having acted yet would leave the differentiating work exactly where it was, in what they would do, merely known in advance. A writer arguing that the purpose rests on the Caller does not reach for a circumstance that leaves the human side deciding.

That does not by itself dispose of foreseen *faith*, since Paul's stated exclusion here is works and he distinguishes faith from works. What it does is make the pre-birth clause evidence about the ground rather than a remark about sequence — and 9:16 then extends the exclusion past works to willing.

**And the criterion model has a shape that answers itself.** Put it at full strength. God decrees: I will elect those who believe. The decree is God's, it is not conditioned on anything, and it is the same decree for every man. Now ask why A is elected and B is not. The decree cannot be the answer, because the decree covers both equally. The answer is that A believed. So on this model the term that distinguishes A from B is A's faith, and the answer to the contrast question has a human term in it.

Paul's answer to that question has no human term in it. He denies two and names one, and the one he names is God who shows mercy.

An objector will say a criterion can sit underneath a source-statement without being mentioned in it. That is the move step 2b answers comparatively: 9:11–12 names the Caller against works; 9:15 states no criterion; 9:16 places the stated dependence on God; 9:18 twice makes God's will distributive; 9:19 adds only the weaker observation that Paul does not restore a criterion; and 11:7 restates the successful side as the elect. No one datum deductively excludes a subordinate criterion. The case is that the criterion model must insert the same unmarked source/criterion distinction across the whole sequence.

Middle knowledge is the same move made carefully. On that account God knows what each possible man would freely do under each set of circumstances, and creates and places accordingly. But *what Jacob would do* is a fact about Jacob's responding, and therefore responsive by the definition at the front. It occupies the same slot in the explanation that Jacob's actual faith would occupy, and the sentence is indifferent to whether the fact is actual or counterfactual.

An advocate may reply that a counterfactual of creaturely freedom is not a standing property of the man, so it is not the kind of fact the thesis classifies. The classification does not ask that. It asks what the proposed fact states, and what this one states is whether Jacob believes.

**One thing the argument does not need.** It does not need faith and *thelein* to be the same concept. An objector may say faith is receptive trust rather than the willing and running Paul names, and he may be right about the words. The argument runs on 2a and on the shape above: works are excluded by 9:11 and 11:6; faith is marked by Paul as distinct from works and is tested on its own; and putting faith in the criterion slot supplies a human term to an answer Paul gives without one.

**4a. The foreknowledge texts, which the objector will bring here.**

Two verses are the textual foundation of the foreseen-faith position, and a paper that argues against foreseen faith as the ground cannot leave them alone.

"Those whom he **foreknew** he also predestined to be conformed to the image of his Son." (Rom 8:29) "Chosen and destined by God the Father, **and sanctified by the Spirit** to be obedient to Jesus Christ." (1 Pet 1:1–2, where the NRSV runs the two verses together; the Greek has *kata prognōsin theou patros* — according to the foreknowledge of God the Father, which the NRSV renders "destined by")

Neither says *foreseen faith*. Both say *foreknew* and *foreknowledge*, and the objector supplies the object. That is the move to notice: he reads *proegnō* as knowing a fact about the man — that he would believe — and then grounds the predestining in that fact.

Two things tell against it, and neither requires settling what *proegnō* means in general.

First, the object in Romans 8:29 is persons, not propositions. Paul writes *hous* — whom he foreknew — not *what* he foreknew about them. That does not make the foreseen-faith reading ungrammatical, since one can foreknow a person under a description. It means the reading is not stated by the text and requires a further inference about what God foreknew concerning those persons.

Second, and what matters for this paper's classification: even granting the objector's reading, it lands him in fork two. If the predestining rests on the man's foreseen believing, then the answer to why this man rather than another has a responsive human term in it. Step 4 has already said why the timing of God's knowing does not change that, and why the criterion model supplies the term whatever produced the faith. So the foreknowledge texts do not open an escape from the trilemma. They name a place inside fork two. Whether faith occupies that place is what step 2b weighs, and the foreknowledge texts do not answer it either way.

Two more texts an objector may bring here. Romans 8:28 names the beneficiaries as "those who love God," and Paul says elsewhere that "anyone who loves God is known by him" (1 Cor 8:3). So, the objector says, God foreknows the ones who love him. Paul's own clauses run the other way. Romans 8:28 goes on: "who are called according to his purpose." The lovers of God are identified by God's calling according to his purpose. And Paul corrects the order of knowing himself: "Now, however, that you have come to know God, or rather to be known by God" (Gal 4:9). The *or rather* makes God's knowing the governing fact. First Corinthians 8:3 says who is known. It does not say that the loving came first.

**5. The enabling-grace reply.**

The strongest form of fork two does not say faith is unaided. It says grace is prior, sufficient, and given to both; that it enables faith without producing it; and that the man's response is what differs.

State it at full strength, because it is a common form of the objection. Then put the dilemma, which does not depend on a stipulation the objector can refuse.

Either the grace given to the two men differed, or it did not.

If it differed, ask what grounds the differing grace — and then ask the second question, because the first does not answer it.

Suppose God gave more to one *because of* something about him: a disposition, a readiness, a foreseen response. That grounds the difference in grace, and grounds it in the man. It does not yet put the objector on fork two, because the ground of the grace and the ground of the election are different questions. God could elect A unconditionally and afterward give him more grace on account of something in him; the human fact would explain the grace and explain nothing about the election, and the thesis would stand untouched.

So put the second question to him. Is the election contingent on that disposition, or on the grace it drew, or on the faith that followed? If it is, that is fork two, and the human fact is in the contrast slot. If it is not, then the human-grounded difference in grace is beside the point, and he has conceded the thesis while holding a view about grace that this paper does not dispute.

Now the other branch. Suppose God gave more to one for no reason in the man. Then the difference *in grace* lies wholly on God's side — and the same caution applies in reverse. That settles the ground of the differing grace and does not by itself settle the ground of the election. An objector can grant that God gave the grace freely and still say God elects those whose faith, the faith his own grace produced, meets the condition. Then the faith is the criterion of the election, and that is fork two, regardless of where the faith came from. Steps 2a through 4 classify it there on that ground: a responsive human fact in the contrast slot is a responsive human fact in the contrast slot whether God produced it or not. Whether faith occupies fork two is weighed at 2b. What follows from God's producing the faith, taken with the other papers of this series, is the synthesis's subject.

So differentiated grace does not escape the trilemma and does not by itself enter it either. Whichever way the grace went, the question that decides the thesis is the one asked twice above: is the election contingent on something the man does?

If the grace did not differ, then the response differs. Keep the two questions apart here, because they came apart in the branch above and they come apart again in this one. What accounts for the response-difference is not what this step asks. Whether the response is caused, agent-caused, or brute, the question is what the election is contingent on.

So: if the man's response functions as the qualifying condition his election depends on, that is fork two, whatever produced the response — and a brute response is no escape, since God may decree that he elects those who in fact believe, which makes the believing the condition however it arose. If the response does not function as such a condition, the objector has conceded the thesis.

Step 6 takes up a position that tries to avoid the branch altogether — identical mercy to all, with the difference relocated to the believing and no discriminating election left to ground. Declining to answer is not a further option.

**The merit reply, and why it misses.** An objector will say faith is not a meritorious work. Grant it without argument — Paul says as much, and contesting it would put this paper at odds with Romans 4. It is also beside the point. The question is not whether the act earns wages. It is whether the act is what explains why mercy falls on this man and not that one. A non-meritorious human fact in that slot is still a human fact in that slot, and 9:16 speaks to what the matter rests on, not to whether the man deserved it.

The narrowing at the front does not supply this escape either, and the two lines should not be confused. *Responsive* is not the same line as *meritorious*. Faith is non-meritorious and it is responsive, since it is the man's own act, and so it stands squarely inside the class the thesis denies. What the narrowing sets aside is a property the man does not perform at all, and faith is not one.

An objector may answer that the response is a condition rather than a ground. Paul's own idiom will not carry that weight here. He can say the promise is of faith so that it may be according to grace (Rom 4:16), which makes faith the mode of receiving and keeps the giving gracious. That is how the promise is received. It is not a statement that the receiving explains the giving. Romans 9:16 concerns what the matter rests on, and it pairs willing with running.

**5a. Chosen through belief — the remaining text an objector will bring.**

"God chose you as the first fruits for salvation **through sanctification by the Spirit and through belief in the truth**." (2 Thess 2:13) The choosing and the believing stand in one sentence, with *through* between them. An objector will say this is election through faith, stated.

The objection has a grammatical form worth stating at full strength, because it is the strongest one available to the criterion model. *En hagiasmō pneumatos kai pistei alētheias* — in sanctification of the Spirit and belief of the truth — may modify *salvation* or it may modify *chose*. Brian Abasciano argues for the second, against Bill Mounce, on three grounds: that prepositional phrases ordinarily modify verbs, that Greek word order is weak evidence of attachment, and that the Septuagintal occurrences of *salvation* followed by *en* attach the phrase to the verb rather than to the noun.[^3] Mounce takes the phrase with *salvation*. So the objector may fairly say: if the phrase modifies *chose*, faith is grammatically attached to the act of choosing, which is the positive marker step 2b says is absent.

**Grant him the attachment.** Suppose the phrase modifies *chose*. The verse then says God chose them through sanctification by the Spirit and through belief in the truth. What the objector needs is that the choosing depended on the belief — that a man who did not believe would not have been chosen, whether the belief is taken as actual or as foreseen.

**He does not need "because of."** The Greek is ἐν with the dative, and its ordinary sense here is means or setting; the NRSV renders it "through." *Through* is not *because of*, and that is true. It does not settle the question. A means can still be a condition. A man hired through an interview was hired by that means, and his hiring still depended on it. So on the instrumental reading the objector's case is that belief is the means by which a man comes to be among the chosen, and a means of that kind is a condition.

**Nor does the single preposition settle it.** The phrase is ἐν ἁγιασμῷ πνεύματος καὶ πίστει ἀληθείας. One preposition governs both nouns, so whatever relation it states, it states for both. But a joint means that includes belief still includes belief. If the choosing depended on the Spirit's sanctifying and on the man's believing together, it depended partly on a responsive fact, and fork two takes in partial grounds.

**So the question is which relation the phrase states, and the verse alone does not decide it.** On one reading, sanctification and belief are the means by which the choosing is carried out: God chose them for salvation, and brings them to it by sanctifying them and by their believing. On the other, they are the means by which a man comes to be among the chosen. Both are readings of the same words.

**Two texts favor the first.** Peter uses the same phrase, ἐν ἁγιασμῷ πνεύματος, with an election word: "who have been chosen and destined by God the Father and sanctified by the Spirit to be obedient to Jesus Christ" (1 Pet 1:1–2). In the Greek the obedience is εἰς ὑπακοήν, the goal of the election. Peter calls that response "obedience to the truth" (1:22). So where an apostle puts election, the Spirit's sanctifying, and the response to the truth together, the response is what the election is for.

Paul's next sentence reads, "For this purpose he called you through our proclamation of the good news, so that you may obtain the glory of our Lord Jesus Christ" (2 Thess 2:14). They believed through the proclamation. Paul puts that calling under the purpose God chose them for. So the belief belongs to carrying the choice out. The verse places it under the choice's purpose; it does not by itself date the two.

The contrast just before the verse pairs unbelief with perishing (2:10–12) and belief with salvation (2:13). That pairs belief with being saved, which this paper grants. It does not by itself pair belief with being chosen.

**What this step establishes.** The attachment of the phrase to *chose* is a warranted grammatical reading, and the paper grants it. On that attachment the verse is the nearest text an objector has to faith joined with a choosing. It does not state that the choosing depended on the belief, and 1 Peter 1:2 and 2 Thessalonians 2:14 favor the reading on which belief carries out the choice. So the verse gives the objector an arguable reading, not a stated criterion. It is weighed with the rest of the case for faith at step 2b, and the result is comparative.

**6. Mercy shown to all, without distribution.**

One position remains, and it does not sit on any of the three forks. It denies that there is a discriminatory mercy to be grounded.

The position: God shows mercy to all. The cross, the gospel, the drawing — real mercy, really shown, to everyone. Its texts are 1 Timothy 2:4, that God "desires everyone to be saved and to come to the knowledge of the truth," and 2 Peter 3:9, that he is "not wanting any to perish, but all to come to repentance." Romans itself supplies one: "For God has imprisoned all in disobedience so that he may be merciful to all" (11:32) — and it should be named here, since it stands in the letter this paper argues from. In its own context the *all* is Jews and Gentiles as groups, which is the subject of chapters 9 through 11, and it does not undo the distributive *whom* of chapter 9. None of the three is disputed here. Each bears on how wide the class is, which step 6a takes up only far enough to place one man outside it; nothing in that step requires reading these verses against their own contexts. The position continues: without that universal mercy nobody would believe. So the matter does rest on God who shows mercy, and the second clause of 9:16 is satisfied. What differs between the two men afterward is their believing, and the objector may make that brute, or free, or whatever he prefers.

Be exact about what this is and is not. It is not a version of fork three. Fork three says a person's election has no ground; this position says there is no discriminating election at all — the mercy was the same for everyone — and relocates the difference to the believing. That is the other question, the one steps 1 through 5 keep separate from this one, and making the response-difference brute settles nothing about the ground of an election, since on this model there is no discriminating election to ground.

So the position is answered not by the trilemma but by asking whether Paul's argument is about an undistributed mercy.

Look at what 9:16 is concluding from. Verse 15 quotes Exodus: "I will have mercy on **whom** I have mercy." Verse 18 states it as a contrast: "he has mercy on whomever he chooses, and he hardens the heart of whomever he chooses." The sentences are about the objects of mercy, not its availability. A universal reading can give *whom* a universal extension, so the objection is not that the word becomes meaningless. It is that identical, undistinguishing mercy does not explain the distributive force the word carries in this argument — an argument drawn from one brother and not the other, and continued at 9:18 with a contrast between mercy and hardening.

And 9:16 opens with *ara oun* — so then. It is a conclusion drawn from the cases of Isaac and Jacob, which are cases of one and not the other. A reading on which the mercy was identical for both explains nothing about the cases it is drawn from.

**6a. At least one man was passed over in the designation.**

Earlier versions of this paper declined to argue this. The decision is reversed, and the reason should be stated before the argument.

The two-man scene is the paper's test for a condition. A condition shows itself where one man meets it and another does not. If every man were elect there would be no second man, and the test would have no case to run on. The per-instance reading at 2a answers that by a different route, so the thesis does not hang on this step. But a test with a possibly empty domain is worth less than a test with a case in it, and the case can be had.

**The argument is about one man.** Establishing that a single person was passed over is enough, because a single person supplies the contrast. Nothing here requires a count, a proportion, or a doctrine of reprobation.

**Hebrews speaks of Esau personally.** "See to it that no one fails to obtain the grace of God; that no root of bitterness springs up and causes trouble, and through it many become defiled. See to it that no one becomes like Esau, an immoral and godless person, who sold his birthright for a single meal. You know that later, when he wanted to inherit the blessing, he was rejected, for he found no chance to repent, even though he sought the blessing with tears." (Heb 12:15–17)

The passage is a warning built on his case, and it names him.

**The verb is strong and the rejection attaches to the blessing.** The clause is θέλων κληρονομῆσαι τὴν εὐλογίαν ἀπεδοκιμάσθη — *wanting to inherit the blessing, he was rejected*. ἀπεδοκιμάσθη is the aorist passive of ἀποδοκιμάζω, to reject after testing, the verb used of the stone the builders rejected. What he was rejected from is named in the clause itself.

The closing words, μετανοίας γὰρ τόπον οὐχ εὗρεν καίπερ μετὰ δακρύων ἐκζητήσας αὐτήν, are disputed: αὐτήν could take either εὐλογίαν or μετάνοιαν, since both are feminine, and the pronoun's accusative comes from its own clause rather than from its antecedent. The NRSV resolves it as "the blessing." Nothing here turns on that dispute. What he sought with tears is a separate question from what he was rejected from, and only the second is used.

**The blessing in view is the covenant one, because Esau received the other kind.** Hebrews says Isaac blessed both sons: "By faith Isaac invoked blessings for the future on Jacob **and Esau**" (11:20). Genesis gives Esau's — away from the fatness of the earth, by the sword you shall live, you shall serve your brother (27:39–40). So Esau was not left unblessed.

That strongly identifies what *the* blessing means at 12:17. If Esau received a paternal blessing and was still rejected when he sought to inherit the blessing, the thing he was rejected from is not a paternal blessing as such. It is the one Jacob took.

**And the blessing Jacob took is the Abrahamic one.** This is an inference rather than a naming, and it should be classified as one: Genesis 28:4 is a later and separate speech from the blessing of chapter 27. Three things support it.

The chapter 27 blessing already carries the Abrahamic formula in its own words: "Cursed be everyone who curses you, and blessed be everyone who blesses you" (27:29) is Genesis 12:3. Esau names birthright and blessing together as what Jacob took from him: "He took away my birthright; and look, now he has taken away my blessing" (27:36). He names two injuries, and the paper does not claim the two words mean one thing. And when Isaac blesses Jacob knowingly and sends him away, he names what he is conferring: "May he give to you **the blessing of Abraham**, to you and to your offspring with you, so that you may take possession of the land where you now live as an alien—land that God gave to Abraham" (28:4).

So the birthright Esau sold, the blessing he lost, and the blessing of Abraham belong to one inheritance, which the narrative names three ways.

**And the Abrahamic inheritance terminates in salvation.** That is the companion paper's result, imported at the front of this one.

Put the four together. Esau wanted to inherit the thing whose terminus is salvation, and he was rejected from inheriting it. So one named man was passed over in the designation, and the contrast the argument tests by is not empty.

That is a claim about the designation, not about Esau's final state. The companion paper shows that the designated heir's inheritance ends in salvation. It does not show that everyone passed over in a patriarchal designation is shut out of every later share in the promise through Christ, and this paper does not claim it. The Ishmael paragraph below needs the same distinction.

**What this does not claim.** It does not say what Esau's final state is. It does not say how many were passed over, or that any particular other person was. It does not say why Esau was rejected — that is the hardening question, bracketed at the front and raised by Paul at 9:19. And it does not make Esau's rejection the ground of anyone's election.

**What an objector can do with it.** He can dispute that the blessing Esau sought is the Abrahamic inheritance rather than a lesser paternal blessing, though Genesis 28:4 names it. He can dispute the companion paper's terminus argument, which is where that fight belongs. Or he can read ἀπεδοκιμάσθη as rejection from that one occasion rather than from the inheritance itself — in which case he owes an account of why Hebrews builds a warning against falling away on a man who lost only a paternal blessing.

**And one route is closed to him.** He cannot answer by way of Ishmael. Ishmael is excluded from the covenant designation in Genesis 17, and Paul uses him in Galatians 4 as the type of the flesh-born. But God blesses him, makes him fruitful, promises him a great nation (17:20), and is with the boy (21:20). Nothing renders a verdict on the man. The argument above runs on Esau because Esau is the case Scripture decides, and it needs no other.

**7. So the difference comes from God.**

Three forks, and one position that stands outside them. Take the outside position first: step 6 answered the claim that there is no personal election to ground at all, from the distributive force of Paul's *whom* and from the cases the conclusion is drawn out of; step 11 supplies the antecedent-personal-election argument it depends on.

Then the forks. Fork three falsifies the second clause: if the matter rests on nothing, it does not rest on God who shows mercy. Fork two falls under the first clause for works, for willing and running, and for any responsive candidate without a text behind it, whether actual, foreseen, or counterfactual. For faith, fork two is not excluded here; step 2b shows it is the less supported reading. So this paper reaches fork one for every candidate but faith, and a comparative result for faith. The second clause tells you what occupies fork one: of God who shows mercy. The logic gives no-responsive-qualifying-condition as far as the anti-criterion premise reaches; the verse gives God.

**7a. The direction the explanations run, which is the same everywhere.**

Step 2b compared readings passage by passage. There is a pattern across the whole corpus that no single passage shows, and it is worth stating on its own because it is the strongest thing this paper has against the criterion model.

**Sort every relevant sentence by which way the explanation runs.**

God's act explaining the man's response: "Everything that the Father gives me will come to me" (John 6:37). "As many as had been destined for eternal life became believers" (Acts 13:48). "You do not believe, because you do not belong to my sheep" (John 10:26). "Those whom he predestined he also called; and those whom he called he also justified; and those whom he justified he also glorified" (Rom 8:30). "Israel failed to obtain what it was seeking. The elect obtained it, but the rest were hardened" (Rom 11:7).

The man's response explaining something: "righteousness through faith" (Rom 9:30). "You are all children of God through faith" (Gal 3:26). "They were broken off because of their unbelief, but you stand only through faith" (Rom 11:20).

**Now look at what the second list is about.** Attaining righteousness. Standing as a son. Standing in the olive tree. Being grafted back.

Not one of them is about being elected. The nearest is 2 Thessalonians 2:13, where step 5a grants that belief may be attached to *chose*. Even there the verse does not say the choosing depended on the belief, and the texts around it favor the reading on which belief carries out the choice.

**And that is the criterion model's problem stated as a pattern rather than as a verse.** It needs one place where a human response explains an election. It has faith explaining attainment, faith explaining sonship-standing, unbelief explaining removal from the tree. It has no text that unambiguously makes faith the reason for a choosing.

The model must also hold the converse, whether or not it says so. If election tracks believing, then not being elected tracks not believing; the complement follows from the criterion and is not a separate commitment. So Romans 11:20's "broken off because of their unbelief" is not an independent text for the model — it is the same claim in negative form, and it explains removal from the tree, which step 12's inference-list entry distinguishes from the Romans 8 set that loses no one.

**State the strength of this exactly.** It is not a deductive exclusion. An objector may say the direction is an artifact of which questions each passage happens to ask. What he cannot do is produce the missing case, and the standard at step 2a is that a proposed criterion needs positive warrant rather than an absence of counterexamples on the other side. This step says the absence of an unambiguous case runs one way and has run one way across every passage either side has brought.

**7b. Paul asks the same question and gives the same answer.**

"Consider your own call, brothers and sisters... God chose what is foolish in the world to shame the wise; God chose what is weak in the world to shame the strong; God chose what is low and despised in the world... so that no one might boast in the presence of God. **He is the source of your life in Christ Jesus**." (1 Cor 1:26–30)

Three times God chose, and then the reason the boasting is excluded: the believer's being in Christ is from God. Five verses earlier Paul says God "decided, through the foolishness of our proclamation, to save those who believe" (1:21) — which names believers as the object of saving, and names the means, and says nothing about what grounds the choosing. The two verses sit together without tension. That is the difference question answered in Paul's own words, and the answer is the one step 7 reaches by elimination.

**And Paul puts the difference question itself in the same letter.** "For who sees anything different in you? What do you have that you did not receive? And if you received it, why do you boast as if it were not a gift?" (1 Cor 4:7) The first clause is τίς γάρ σε διακρίνει — who distinguishes you? — which is the contrast question of step 2a asked in Paul's own words, and what he points to in answering it is what was received rather than what the man brought.

Keep its weight where it belongs. The immediate subject is Corinthian boasting over teachers, so the verse is not a statement about the ground of election, and this paper does not use it as one. What it supplies is that Paul asks the difference question at all, and that when he asks it he answers from the side of the giver.

**The objection this text invites.** An attacker will say the passage names human properties tied to the choosing by purpose clauses. God chose what is foolish *in order to* shame the wise; what is weak *in order to* shame the strong. Weakness belongs to the man, and Paul attaches a purpose to the choosing of weak objects. If God chose weak objects because their weakness serves that purpose, then weakness is relevant to why those objects were selected — and step 2b says in so many words that a purpose the chooser formed independently does not stop him from applying a criterion to the objects.

Two answers. They are independent, and the first is the one that disposes of the objection.

**First, the property is not of the kind the thesis denies.** Weakness, foolishness, and low birth are not responsive facts. None of them states the man's responding; a man does not perform his weakness. The thesis denies that a responsive fact is the condition a person's election depends on, and the front matter says that it leaves non-responsive properties open. So grant the attacker his entire construction — God formed a purpose to shame the strong, weak objects serve it, weakness is therefore relevant to their selection — and nothing he has said contradicts what this paper claims. The step 2b principle he is invoking is correctly stated and lands outside the thesis.

Say plainly what that costs and what it does not. It costs the sentence *nothing whatever about the man bears on his election*, which this paper does not assert and never argued. It leaves foreseen faith exactly where steps 1 through 7 left it, because faith is the man's own act and is therefore responsive.

**Second, the passage does not state the relation the attacker needs, and its grammar points away from it.** This answer is comparative and does not refute his reading; it is here because the passage is worth reading accurately, not because the first answer needs help.

Paul's objects are neuter plurals: τὰ μωρὰ τοῦ κόσμου, τὰ ἀσθενῆ τοῦ κόσμου, τὰ ἀγενῆ τοῦ κόσμου, τὰ ἐξουθενημένα — the foolish things of the world, the weak things, the low-born things, the things counted as nothing. The masculine was available, and Paul uses it in the same sentence for the other side: what God shames at 1:27a is τοὺς σοφούς, the wise men. So what he says God chose is the kind. The genitive *tou kosmou* marks the standard by which the kind is reckoned, and the standard is the world's appraisal rather than a property simply possessed.

**The cost this answer pays, stated here rather than left for an objector to find.** One verse earlier Paul describes the called themselves in the masculine: οὐ πολλοὶ σοφοὶ κατὰ σάρκα, οὐ πολλοὶ δυνατοί, οὐ πολλοὶ εὐγενεῖς — not many wise according to the flesh, not many powerful, not many well-born, all three nominative masculine plural. So the same people are described as men at 1:26 and as things at 1:27. An objector will say the neuter is a generalizing idiom for a class of persons, and that 1:26 shows Paul has persons in view throughout.

Grant that the idiom exists. What it does not account for is the distribution. Every masculine in the stretch attaches either to describing the called or to naming who is shamed; the neuter appears only where ἐξελέξατο is the verb, and it appears there four times. So the neuter is a marker and not a proof, which is what this second answer claims to be.

What the attacker's relation requires is that a given man's election was contingent on his weakness — that had he not been weak, he would not have been elected. What Paul supplies is a kind, designated by the world's reckoning, with a purpose stated for the choosing of that kind. The contingency is supplied by the model rather than marked by the sentence, which is the test step 2b applies to faith.

**One escape this paper does not take.** It would be open to say that the choosing of 1:27–28 is some other act than the antecedent salvific determination under review. The paper does not say that. The people in view are the called, and 1:30 has them in Christ Jesus. The answer above does not need the escape and does not use it.

**One note on what neither answer uses.** It would be easy to argue that weakness cannot ground the choice because no one can boast of being weak. That argument is not available here, because step 5 denies its converse: a non-meritorious fact can still be a qualifying condition. Non-boastworthy does not entail non-grounding any more than non-meritorious does.

James has the same shape and the same objection available. "Has not God chosen the poor in the world to be **rich in faith** and to be heirs of the kingdom that he has promised to those who love him?" (Jas 2:5) An objector will say poverty is a human property named with the choosing. Poverty is non-responsive, so the first answer above covers it directly. The grammar points the same way as in 1 Corinthians. The Greek is ἐξελέξατο τοὺς πτωχοὺς τῷ κόσμῳ πλουσίους ἐν πίστει καὶ κληρονόμους τῆς βασιλείας — a double accusative, with *rich in faith* and *heirs* as predicate accusatives further defining *the poor*. There is no verb *to be*; the English versions supply it. The predicate accusatives describe the chosen as rich in faith and as heirs. They do not say when the chosen became so, and the grammar alone does not settle it. Nor do they put faith on the near side of the choice. The last clause, "that he has promised to those who love him," names who receive the promised kingdom. It does not say what the choosing depended on.

The same contrast appears in this very letter, in Paul's own not-this-but-that form: "So too at the present time there is a remnant, chosen by grace. But if it is by grace, it is no longer on the basis of works." (Rom 11:5–6) The next sentence names who obtained and who did not: "Israel failed to obtain what it was seeking. The elect obtained it, but the rest were hardened." (11:7)

## Further evidence: order, and the source of the new birth

The trilemma is an elimination. Two texts state an order directly rather than leaving it to be inferred, and a third places the new birth on God's side without settling its relation to faith. None of them by itself excludes foreseen faith; see the dependency note in the front matter.

**8. Jesus supplies order evidence in the sheep saying.**

"But you do not believe, because you do not belong to my sheep." (John 10:26)

On the ordinary causal reading, he does not say they are not his sheep because they do not believe; their not being among the sheep is given as the reason for the unbelief. An evidentiary reading is possible — their unbelief shows that they are not sheep — and the inference list records it. Verse 27 nevertheless describes hearing and following as what the sheep do: "My sheep hear my voice. I know them, and they follow me." The text therefore supplies order evidence, but the elimination in steps 1 through 7 does not depend on settling the causal-versus-evidentiary question.

**8a. And Jesus states whose initiative the choosing was.**

"You did not choose me but I chose you. And I appointed you to go and bear fruit." (John 15:16)

The context is the Eleven, and the sentence continues into their mission, so the verse does not travel as far as Romans 9. What it supplies is narrow. Jesus names both directions of choosing in one sentence and affirms his own. That is evidence of initiative. It does not date the disciples' faith relative to his choosing. The choosing in view may be the appointment of the Twelve, since Jesus uses the same verb of the Twelve with Judas among them: "Did I not choose you, the twelve? Yet one of you is a devil" (John 6:70). So this step corroborates initiative and claims nothing more.

**9. Luke states it in the same direction.**

"And as many as had been destined for eternal life became believers." (Acts 13:48)

On the ordinary passive reading, the appointing is antecedent and the believing follows. The other order was available and was not used. The participle *tetagmenoi* is formally middle or passive, and the inference list argues for the passive; this step is stated at that strength and no more.

Finish the paragraph, since an opponent will. Two verses earlier Paul tells the Jews who rejected the word that they "judge yourselves to be unworthy of eternal life" (13:46). So Luke puts human rejection and divine appointment in one passage without treating them as competing explanations of the same fact — the rejecters judge themselves, the appointed believe. The paper claims what Luke's sentence at 13:48 says about the second group, not a theory reconciling it with the first.

**10. John places the new birth on God's side.**

Those who become children of God are "born, not of blood or of the will of the flesh or of the will of man, but of God." (John 1:13)

Finish the citation, since an opponent will. The preceding verse reads: "But to all who received him, who believed in his name, he gave power to become children of God" (1:12). Receiving and believing stand before the giving of the right. John sets that sentence and the next one side by side and offers no reconciliation of them, which is the same thing Luke does at Acts 13:46 and 13:48. The paper claims what verse 13 states about the birth, not a theory that relates it to verse 12.

So this step is not an order text and is not used as one. Steps 8 and 9 put belonging and appointment before believing; this one puts the new birth on God's side of the ledger and says nothing about where it stands relative to faith.

*Not of the will of man* denies that the man's will produces the new birth. State its force accurately: a foreseen-faith reader can agree with that sentence, since he holds the man believes and God alone performs the birth. So John 1:13 does not by itself refute step 5. What it establishes is that the birth is God's act and no part of it is the man's production. That source claim stands alongside the order evidence of steps 8 and 9; it does not add a further birth-before-faith sequence.

## The willing texts, taken together

A paper that turns on *not of the one willing* owes an account of the places Scripture puts willing on the man's side, and there are several.

"Yet you refuse to come to me to have life." (John 5:40 — οὐ θέλετε ἐλθεῖν, literally *you are not willing to come*, the same verb as Romans 9:16.) "How often have I desired to gather your children together... and you were not willing!" (Matt 23:37) "Let anyone who wishes take the water of life as a gift." (Rev 22:17 — *ho thelōn*, positively, of salvation.) And the verse every reader has: "everyone who believes in him may not perish but may have eternal life" (John 3:16). And in the chapter this paper quotes for the heart-circumcision: "See, I have set before you today life and prosperity, death and adversity" (Deut 30:15), and "**Choose life**" (30:19).

One more belongs here, from the letter this paper argues from, and an objector will notice if it is missing. Two chapters after the argument under review Paul quotes Isaiah against Israel: "All day long I have held out my hands to a disobedient and contrary people." (Rom 10:21) Hands held out, and a people who would not. That is the sharpest statement in Romans of God reaching and man refusing, and this paper grants it as it stands.

Finish the pair, since the verse before it runs the other way and quoting one without the other would be the sort of thing this paper charges others with. "I have been found by those who did not seek me; I have shown myself to those who did not ask for me." (Rom 10:20, quoting Isa 65:1) The Gentiles who attained were not seeking. So within four verses Paul says the attaining was not produced by the attainer's pursuit and the refusal was the refuser's own. Both stand, and neither puts the believer's willing in the slot 9:16 empties.

One answer covers them. Each attributes the unbeliever's not-coming to his own unwillingness, or commands the man to choose, or opens the invitation to whoever wills. The paper grants every one of those. It grants that the man wills, that the unbeliever's refusal is his own, and that the invitation is open.

None of them says what this thesis denies. None says that the believer's coming is grounded in his willing as against his neighbor's. John 3:16 states who has life — everyone who believes — and the paper grants it without qualification; a statement of who has life is not a statement of what grounds the choosing, which is the same distinction the paper draws at John 6:40 and at Romans 10. John 5:40 and John 6:44 stand in one Gospel — you are unwilling to come, and no one can come unless drawn — and on this paper's account both are true of the same men. A command to choose states what is required, not what explains why one man does it. An invitation to whoever wills states who may come, not why this one comes.

## The class this concerns

**11. The class is the one the companion paper established, and Romans 9 applies its election principle to it.**

Believers are Abraham's offspring and heirs according to the promise (Gal 3:29), adopted as sons (Gal 4:4–5), and predestined to that adoption before the foundation of the world (Eph 1:4–5). Paul states in the same passage what the predestining is according to, and whose will stands behind it: they were "destined according to the **purpose** of him who accomplishes all things according to **his** counsel and will" (Eph 1:11). The NRSV's *destined* renders προορισθέντες, predestined. The predestining is κατὰ πρόθεσιν — according to the purpose. The following phrase, κατὰ τὴν βουλὴν τοῦ θελήματος αὐτοῦ, describes the God who works all things, so it does not modify the predestining directly. What it establishes is the point needed here: the *thelēma* named in the sentence is God's. The companion paper reaches that class without using Romans 9 at all.

Paul states the same shape a second time, in another letter and without Romans 9's vocabulary: God "saved us and called us with a holy calling, not according to our works but according to his own purpose and grace. This grace was given to us in Christ Jesus before the ages began." (2 Tim 1:9) Three features of the architecture argued here stand in one sentence — saving and calling predicated of the same objects, works excluded by name, and a God-side term named in their place — and it supplies a temporal marker Romans 9 does not: πρὸ χρόνων αἰωνίων, before times eternal.

State its reach exactly. What it excludes is works, and works are already excluded by name at 9:11–12 and 11:6. It says nothing about faith. So it is a second witness to the shape of the explanation rather than a further exclusion, and this step uses it as one.

**Start with what the chapter was written to answer.** Paul opens with grief over his kinsmen: he could wish himself accursed for their sake (9:3). Then the question that organizes everything after it — it is not as though the word of God had failed, for not all Israelites truly belong to Israel (9:6). The problem is unbelief among people who heard, and whether God's word failed on account of it.

Isaac and Jacob are how Paul answers that question. They are not a side exhibit about two dead men. Isaac is the first case, at 9:7–9: "it is not the children of the flesh who are the children of God, but the children of the promise are counted as descendants." That sentence builds the class-name. Jacob is the second case, and 9:16 is the conclusion drawn from both.

So the jurisdiction of 9:16 is the question 9:6 posed. A reader who says the verse governs two patriarchs and expires when the twins leave the page has to explain what it then contributes to an argument about why Paul's kinsmen do not believe. And Galatians 4:28 puts the churches in the class-name Paul built to answer it: you, my friends, are children of the promise, like Isaac.

**Is the election in view personal and antecedent?** Before the scope question, a prior one — and the front matter has already narrowed whom it is for. The aggregate and corporate-with-personal-election models concede an antecedent personal determination and dispute only its ground, which steps 1 through 7 handle. What is left is the corporate-first advocate, who grants everything above and says the data establish personal *mercy* and personal *calling* without establishing an antecedent personal determination of a person's salvific destiny. On his model God elects the covenant people, persons participate in that people, and mercy and calling operate on persons within it. Election, calling, and mercy are three relations; they can all run continuously through the chapter without being one act on one object. Vocabulary continuity does not by itself close that, and this step should not pretend it does.

What closes it is a chain in which an antecedent act and a personal outcome have the same objects.

"We know that all things work together for good for those who love God, who are **called according to his purpose**. For those whom he foreknew he also predestined to be conformed to the image of his Son... And those whom he predestined he also called; and those whom he called he also justified; and those whom he justified he also glorified." (Rom 8:28–30)

Note the two terms in verse 28, because they recur. *Purpose* and *calling* stand together there, immediately before the chain; and at 9:11 the same pair stands with election — "God's **purpose** of **election** might continue, not by works but by **his call**" (9:11–12). So the bridge between the two chapters is not built only on 9:24's pronoun. Purpose and calling appear on both sides of it, with election named alongside them in the second.

One relative pronoun runs the whole chain, and the same objects carry from link to link. Justification is personal on every account — "since we are justified by faith, we have peace with God" (5:1). And the objects of the predestining are those same persons: *hous* is masculine plural and takes persons, not a body. Paul does not write that God predestined the people and that persons then share in it. He writes that God predestined *them*.

Three verses later Paul names the same company by election: "Who will bring any charge against God's elect? It is God who justifies." (Rom 8:33) The justified of 8:30 are God's elect of 8:33. So the step from the chain to election does not rest on vocabulary brought in from chapter 9; Paul uses the word in chapter 8 of the same people.

So Romans 8:29–30 supplies exactly what the front matter defined: an antecedent divine determination, with persons as its objects, whose terminus is their glorification. Whether one calls it election is a question about words. It is the object this paper's thesis is about, and the trilemma partitions its ground.

Then 9:24 ties chapter 9's calling to that chain: "including us whom he has **called**, not from the Jews only but also from the Gentiles." The called of 9:24 are the called of 8:30.

**What this does to the corporate model.** Less than one might reach for, and the paper should say so. The corporate advocate need not break the *hous*, and he need not hold that every participant in the wider covenant people is glorified — he may delimit the Romans 8 set as narrowly as he likes and still affirm every link in the chain. That move is open to him and this paper grants it.

What is not open is declining the question. However he delimits that set, its members have been assigned a salvific destiny by an antecedent act of God predicated of them. So: what conditions a person's being among them?

His answer is faith — faith brings a man into the body, and those in the body are predestined to glory. Then the determination assigning that person the destiny is contingent on his faith, since contingent on membership and membership contingent on faith is contingent on faith. That is fork two, and the paper answers it there.

He may reply that God predestines the body and the man merely participates in a destiny already fixed, so no act of predestining *him* is conditioned on anything. That reply needs Paul to have predicated the predestining of the body. He predicated it of persons — *hous he foreknew, these he predestined* — and the chain terminates in a justification and a glorification that are personal on every account.

**One thing this argument does not use.** The chain begins with *foreknew*, which is exactly where the foreseen-faith reader stands. Nothing here rests on it. The chain is cited for the locus of the election — that it is personal and antecedent — and says nothing about what conditions it. That question is answered at 4a, where the foreknowledge texts are taken up on their own terms.

**The scope of Romans 9:16 has to be argued, not asserted.** Showing that believers are Abraham's promise-heirs does not by itself show that Paul's conclusion at 9:16 governs them. That premise is load-bearing here — steps 3 through 7 carry the whole thesis, and they carry it on 9:16 — so it gets its own argument. And this step does a second job named in the front matter: it tests whether the corporate advocate can partition the election of 9:11 from the calling and mercy of 9:15–24. If the passage runs them together on one vocabulary, the partition has no place to be inserted.

*Paul names the category and puts believers in it.* Romans 9:8 calls the true offspring "children of the promise." Galatians 4:28 says to believers: "Now you, my friends, are children of the promise, like Isaac." Same writer, same term, same patriarch used to define it.

*And Romans 9 does not leave that category behind before reaching 9:16 or after it.* The chain runs continuously: children of God and children of promise (9:8), the purpose of election and "his call" (9:11–12), mercy (9:15, 16, 18), objects of mercy prepared beforehand for glory (9:23 — see the note below), "us whom he has called, not from the Jews only but also from the Gentiles" (9:24), "children of the living God" (9:26), and "only a remnant of them will be **saved**" (9:27). Then Paul marks his own conclusion at 9:30 — "What then are we to say?" — and draws it in terms of who attained righteousness: Gentiles who did not pursue it attained it, "that is, righteousness through faith," while Israel did not arrive (9:30–31).

*A note on 9:23.* The clause used above is the second half of a sentence. Verse 22 speaks of vessels of wrath endured with much patience, and verse 23 gives the purpose: in order to make known the riches of his glory for the vessels of mercy. What verse 22 says about the vessels of wrath belongs to questions this paper brackets — who is hardened, and why. Nothing in the continuity argument depends on it. What the chain uses is that Paul describes the positive class as prepared beforehand for glory, and that description stands whatever one makes of the coordinate clause.

Finish the sentence, since an opponent will. Verse 32 gives Paul's reason Israel did not arrive: "Because they did not strive for it on the basis of faith, but as if it were based on works." So in the very argument under review Paul sets faith against works, sharply. A foreseen-faith advocate will use that against any claim that willing and running simply swallow faith.

Grant it. Two answers, and the first is Paul's own frame rather than a distinction this paper supplies.

**Ask which Israel verse 31 is about.** The argument opens at 9:6: "It is not as though the word of God had failed. For not all Israelites truly belong to Israel." Paul separates two referents of the name before he says anything else, and the chapter runs on the separation. When 9:31 says Israel did not succeed in fulfilling the law, the Israel in view is the national body under the descent-referent, considered corporately.

Say that precisely, because the two categories overlap. The national body *contains* the promise-remnant; it is not coextensive with it, and it is not simply the opposite of it. So 9:32 states why the nation in its flesh-defined pursuit did not arrive. It does not state the ground on which the remnant inside that nation became children of promise.

**Paul restates the same contrast himself, and the differentiator he names is not faith.** "What then? Israel failed to obtain what it was seeking. **The elect obtained it, but the rest were hardened.**" (Rom 11:7) That is the remnant-and-rest division inside descent-Israel, in Paul's own words, two chapters later — and the word he uses for the ones who obtained is *elect*. An objector who takes 9:32 as naming the differentiator inside the seeking nation has to say why Paul, restating the same failure, names election instead.

**And the faith-works contrast may be the mark of the distinction rather than its cause.** The children of promise believe the promise; the flesh-defined remainder pursues as if by works. On that reading 9:30–32 describes how the distinction of 9:6–18 shows itself, not what produced it. Nothing in this paper requires that reading, but the objector needs the opposite one and the text does not supply it.

**Then the narrower point, which covers the Gentiles of 9:30.** They did attain, and by faith. So faith is how righteousness is attained — the means — while 9:16 concerns what the calling and the mercy rest on. Paul can name faith as how the called attain, and works as how the nation failed to, without either being the reason the calling fell where it fell. Step 4 has already said the thesis does not need faith to fall under *thelein*; it needs only that a human fact supplied as the reason one man is called and another not is a human fact in the contrast-slot.

Verses 30 through 32 together do not undo verse 16.

One precision about that chain. Verses 25–26 apply Hosea to those called from the Gentiles; verses 27–29 apply Isaiah to Israel's remnant. So "children of the living God" and "only a remnant of them will be saved" do not name one undifferentiated population. The claim is not that every label across 9:23–29 has the same extension. It is that Paul's positive category — called objects of mercy, prepared for glory — takes in both the Gentile sons and the saved Jewish remnant, and that the whole stretch is about salvation. That is what the continuity argument needs.

Three markers run across the stretch that contains 9:16. Sonship: children of God at 9:8, children of the living God at 9:26. Calling: his call at 9:11–12, us whom he has called at 9:24. Mercy: 9:15, 16, 18, and objects of mercy at 9:23.

State the claim at the right strength, given the precision above. It is not that each marker has the same extension at every occurrence — 9:26's sonship is applied to those called from the Gentiles, one part of what fills the category. It is that Paul builds his positive category with one vocabulary and does not switch vocabularies when he moves from the twins to the called. So the class he is reasoning about when he writes *not of the one willing* is the class he goes on to describe as called, prepared for glory, and saved. A reader who restricts 9:16 to Isaac and Jacob has to say where Paul changed populations and why he used the same vocabulary on both sides of the change.

So the exclusion in steps 3 through 7 applies to the class believers belong to, not to two men in Genesis.

**12. The covenant shows the same shape in the founding case.**

God named Isaac as the one with whom he would establish the covenant while Sarah was barren and Isaac unconceived (Gen 17:19, 21). No act of Isaac's was the ground of his naming.

Chronology alone does not exclude foreseen faith — that is step 4's work, not this one's. What the chapter adds is that the covenant's own required act did not select the heir either. Ishmael received the sign in full, on the day it was given, named explicitly (Gen 17:23–26), and was not the one with whom God said he would establish the covenant. Isaac received it at eight days old by his father's hand (Gen 21:4). On neither side does the man's performance do the selecting.

Moses gives the same shape for the nation. "It was not because you were more numerous than any other people that the LORD set his heart on you and chose you—for you were the fewest of all peoples. It was because the LORD loved you and kept the oath that he swore to your ancestors" (Deut 7:7–8). And of the land: "It is not because of your righteousness or the uprightness of your heart that you are going in to occupy their land" (9:5). Keep both at their own scope. The first concerns the choosing of the nation, and the second concerns possession of the land. Neither concerns the personal election of step 11, and the conditions that follow at 7:9–12 govern the covenant's dealings with the nation. The first denies a property the nation had, its size; the second denies what it did, its righteousness. What they add is that where Scripture explains a choosing of God's people, the explanation it gives is on God's side — love and oath — and the human-side candidates it names are denied.

**13. What the covenant requires, God performs.**

"Circumcise, then, the foreskin of your heart, and do not be stubborn any longer." (Deut 10:16) The command is to the man.

"Moreover, the LORD your God will circumcise your heart and the heart of your descendants, so that you will love the LORD your God with all your heart and with all your soul, in order that you may live." (Deut 30:6) The performance is God's, and the *so that* names what it produces.

Same object, same book, commanded of the man and performed by God in the offspring.

**14. The New Testament says the coming is given.**

"Everything that the Father gives me will come to me." (John 6:37) "No one can come to me unless drawn by the Father who sent me." (6:44) "No one can come to me unless it is granted by the Father." (6:65)

Finish the paragraph, since an opponent will. Between the two verses stands 6:40: "This is indeed the will of my Father, that all who see the Son and believe in him may have eternal life." The Father's will is stated there with believing in the description. What 6:40 says is who has eternal life. It does not say what grounds the giving at 6:37, and John places the two sentences four verses apart without reconciling them, as he does at 1:12–13.

One more verse in that stretch, and an objector will want it. "But among you there are some who do not believe. For Jesus knew from the first who were the ones that did not believe... And he said, **For this reason** I have told you that no one can come to me unless it is granted by the Father." (6:64–65) *Knew from the first* is the word a foreseen-faith reader wants. Read what *dia touto* connects. It looks back to the unbelief just observed and gives it as the reason Jesus had spoken as he did — *this is why I told you that no one can come unless it is granted*. So the unbelief is the occasion of the saying.

Be exact about what the saying then establishes, because it is easy to take one step too many. It states a necessary condition: no one can come unless the Father grants it. From a man's not coming, that does not license the inference that the grant was withheld — a necessary condition is not thereby a sufficient one, and the paper would need a further premise about the grant's sufficiency to draw it. Nothing here supplies that premise. What the verse gives is the *cannot*, and that is all this step claims from it.

The verb at 6:44 is ἑλκύω, and there is a long dispute about whether it means to draw irresistibly or to draw in a way that can be refused. The argument does not enter it. What these verses supply is the *cannot* — no one is able to come apart from a divine act — and the statement at 6:37 that those given do come. Neither claim depends on how forcefully the drawing is construed.

"For he has graciously granted you the privilege not only of believing in Christ, but of suffering for him as well." (Phil 1:29)

Peter joins the promise to the calling in one clause at Pentecost: "For the promise is for you, for your children, and for all who are far away, **everyone whom the Lord our God calls to him**." (Acts 2:39) The promise reaches as far as the calling does, and the calling is God's. Like the rest of this step, it does not say why he calls those he calls.

Taken together, these establish two narrower claims: no one comes apart from the Father's drawing or grant, and everyone whom the Father gives to the Son does come. They do not by themselves identify the drawing, the granting, and the giving as the same act, and they do not say why the Father gives those he gives. That is the gap steps 1 through 7 address.

## Conclusion

The discriminatory election and mercy of the promise-class do not rest on the one willing or the one running, but on God who shows mercy. Therefore that election, as Scripture gives it, is not conditioned on works, does not rest on the man's willing or running, and has no responsive condition other than faith with textual warrant. For faith, actual or foreseen, the reading on which faith is not the condition is better supported, and the reading on which it is has not been shown unavailable here. The synthesis completes that step.

**The positive counterpart, which the argument has earned and should state.** Two questions have to be kept apart first, because one sentence covering both would claim more than the argument gives and would run against a text Paul states plainly.

**What constitutes a man an object of God's antecedent determination** is the question this paper has been answering. The answer it supports is: nothing he does. For every candidate but faith it establishes that answer. For faith it establishes that the answer is better supported than its rival.

**How a man comes to stand as a son in Christ** is a different question, and Paul answers it in so many words: "for in Christ Jesus you are all children of God **through faith**" (Gal 3:26 — διὰ τῆς πίστεως, the instrument named). That is granted here without qualification, as it has been granted throughout. The man believes, and through his believing he stands where the promise puts him — an heir according to the promise (3:29), a child of the promise like Isaac (4:28).

With those apart, the positive claim can be stated at its own scope. On the reading this paper supports, the order is not that a man's believing makes him an object of God's electing determination. That determination is antecedent and is God's. His faith is how he comes to stand in Christ as son and heir, and how he receives what was determined for him.

That is why Romans 9:30–32 says what it says without embarrassment. Of course the children of promise attain righteousness by faith. Faith is how they attain. It does not follow that faith is what made them objects of the determination, any more than Isaac's circumcision at eight days made him the promised son.

An objector will say the second question is his position. It is, and it always was granted. The dispute has never been whether a man comes to sonship through faith. It is whether God's antecedent determination of that man turns on it.

John states the same pairing and shows why it must be handled carefully: "Everyone who believes that Jesus is the Christ has been born of God" (1 John 5:1). The perfect γεγέννηται describes the believer as one who stands begotten of God. That joins believing to divine birth in one sentence, which is what this paragraph claims. It does not by itself establish which came first, and the tense should not be asked to carry an order it was not used to state.

Two limits on the positive claim. It is an ordering claim about constitution, not a causal one: nothing here says the birth produces the believing, or that regeneration stands before faith in time, or that the divinely born man necessarily believes because the birth compels it. Those belong to the argument on effectual grace and are not made here. And it does not diminish the faith. The man believes, the believing is his, and by it he receives what was promised.

State the standing of the conclusion exactly, because the argument has two parts with different strengths, and because its range is narrower than a reader may expect.

Begin with the range. What is denied is contingency on a responsive fact — something the man does, wills, or responds with. Whether God's choosing may select on a property the man does not perform at all is a question no step here tests, and the paper neither denies it nor asserts it. That leaves the dispute this paper is about exactly where it was, since faith is the man's own act.

Three things have to be kept apart, because a looser statement would claim more than the argument gives.

The partition is deductively exhaustive. Either something grounds a person's election or nothing does; if something does, it either includes a responsive qualifying condition or it does not. Nothing in that is a judgment call.

Fork three falls deductively once 9:16's second clause is granted. If the matter rests on God who shows mercy, it does not rest on nothing.

Fork two falls deductively once the anti-criterion premise is established — and establishing that premise is not itself deductive. It is the work of steps 2a and 2b: 2a restricts the field of candidates on textual grounds, and 2b argues from the ledger that a responsive criterion can be placed beneath Paul's God-side terms only at a cost the text does not mark. The elimination is valid; the premise it eliminates on is exegetical.

State the result of that premise at its own strength, and separately for the two kinds of candidate. It does not show the criterion model logically incoherent, and no argument here attempts that.

For a responsive candidate with no text behind it, the relation the model needs is stated nowhere in the corpus, entailed by nothing in it, and warranted by no text. Under the evidential rule at the front, a relation in that condition is unavailable as an account of the ground.

For faith the position is different. The relation — election contingent on the man's faith — is stated nowhere and entailed by nothing. But an objector can assemble a case for it from texts that mark related relations, and steps 2b and 5a weigh that case rather than dismiss it. The result is that the reading with no criterion is better supported. That is a comparative result. The paper does not claim here that the faith-criterion reading is unavailable. The synthesis paper takes the further step, using the conclusions of "No One Comes to Christ Unless God First Changes the Heart" and "God Brings Every Heir to Faith and Loses None of Them."

The paper also has not argued the stronger sentence — that the *difference in believing itself* is produced by God's act — and does not argue it here. "No One Comes" and "God Brings Every Heir to Faith" take it up, and the synthesis uses it.

With the companion paper: the election is to salvation, and it is not grounded in works or in the man's willing. With the synthesis: it is not grounded in anything the man does.

## What is inferred, and where to attack

**That the trilemma is exhaustive.** Something grounds a person's election or nothing does; if something, the ground either includes a responsive human qualifying condition or it does not. The forks partition the ground of the election, not the ground of the A/B difference — cutting them on the difference would strand a condition every man satisfies, which is exactly the case fork two exists to catch. The forks are cut on the responsive-condition predicate rather than on in-the-man or on God's act, because both of those leave a gap — the first strands a non-responsive property of the man, which this paper does not claim to have excluded, and the second strands a ground that is neither the man's nor God's. The narrowed predicate also matches the verse the elimination turns on, since willing and running are both responsive. Fork two takes in any account on which a responsive qualifying condition is wholly or partly the ground of a person's election, whether or not some other actual person fails to satisfy it. Joint accounts, agent causation, and a condition every man in fact meets all fall there. Class-election with a faith-conditioned roster falls there on either of its readings. Where personal membership is identified with personal election, faith is what brings a man into the elect and the classification is immediate. Where no personal election is admitted, step 11 shows that Romans 8:29–30 predicates an antecedent determination of persons, so the advocate still owes an account of what conditions a person's being among them — and if the answer is faith-conditioned membership, that is fork two by transitivity.

**That the thesis quantifies over responsive facts only.** This is a limit on what is claimed, and it is stated here because a reader may take the paper for more than it argues. No step tests a non-responsive candidate, and the one passage that raises one — 1 Corinthians 1:26–30 — is answered at 7b by scope rather than by denial. An objector who produces a text making a non-responsive property the condition of an election has not contradicted anything asserted here. He has also not touched foreseen faith, which is responsive.

**That Romans 9:16 governs the class.** This is the load-bearing premise and step 11 argues it rather than borrowing it: Galatians 4:28 places believers in the Romans 9:8 category, and the continuity inside Romans 9 runs sonship, calling, and mercy across the stretch containing 9:16 and on to glory, salvation, and righteousness. A reader who restricts 9:16 to the two covenant cases loses steps 3 through 7 and keeps steps 8 through 10, which is the thesis lost.

**That the named divine source leaves no subordinate human criterion marked in the text, and that the reading with none is better supported than the reading with faith.** Step 2b argues this comparatively rather than by the invalid inference that a source can never apply a rule. The ledger runs through 9:11–12, 9:15, 9:16, 9:17, 9:18, 9:19, and 11:7. The criterion model can accommodate each datum, but only by repeatedly placing an unstated human rule beneath the Caller, mercy, and divine will that Paul actually names. The paper's reading also pays a cost — it distinguishes faith as the means of attaining righteousness from election as the ground of the called objects — but 9:30–32 and 11:7 positively mark those two relations. The claim is comparative explanatory superiority, not that any single verse contains the abstract proposition "God uses no criterion." For faith the paper claims no more than that; the synthesis completes it.

**That 9:16's source-statement answers a contrast-question.** This is the argument's hinge and step 2a states it. The source-reading of the bare genitives is strongly supported by 9:11's explicit *ek* in the same paragraph. The contrast-reading rests on the *ara oun* drawing from two brothers, the distributive *whom* at 9:15 and 9:18, and the mercy-hardening contrast at 9:18. An objector who keeps the source-reading and denies the contrast-reading must say what question 9:16 is the conclusion of — "mercy has a divine source" was not in doubt after 9:15.

**And that the same verse bears a per-instance reading alongside the contrastive one.** Step 2a argues this and the thesis needs it, because a responsive condition every man meets falls under fork two and shows up in a contrast test only when some man fails it. The per-instance reading is the plainer of the two — it takes the sentence as stating what the matter rests on rather than what separates two men — and 9:11–12 and 9:15 supply the same shape without reference to any contrast. An objector who wants the contrastive reading alone has to say why *ara oun* converts a dependence statement into a comparative one.

**That at least one man was passed over in the designation.** Step 6a, from Hebrews 12:17 read with Genesis 28:4 and the companion paper's terminus argument. This is corroborative rather than necessary: it supplies a case for the two-man test, and 2a's per-instance reading covers the same coverage gap without it. Defeating 6a does not defeat the thesis.

**That the responsive candidates with textual standing are works and faith.** The paper does not claim that *willing* and *running* lexically enumerate every responsive act, and it does not claim that every human act other than faith is a work — that inference fails, and faith is the counterexample to it. What it claims is that works are excluded from the basis of choosing by name (9:11–12; 11:6), that faith is marked by Paul as distinct from works (Rom 4:4–5) and so has to be tested separately, and that it is tested at 2b, 4, and 5. A third candidate needs positive textual warrant that it functions as the condition of election; once supplied, and if it is responsive, 2b's generic test applies. Mere conceivability is not a countermodel.

**That the explanations run one way, and that this is a pattern rather than a proof.** Step 7a sorts the relevant sentences by direction and finds every human-response explanation attached to attaining, standing, or grafting, and none attached unambiguously to an election; 2 Thessalonians 2:13 is the nearest candidate and is taken up at 5a. This is cumulative and it is not deductive: an objector may answer that the direction reflects which question each passage asks. What he cannot answer with is an unambiguous instance, and none has been produced by any reviewer or by the published literature the paper cites. The step is stated at that strength and no more.

**That 9:31's Israel is the national body of 9:6's descent-referent.** The chapter opens by separating two referents of the name and is written to defend the separation. The national body contains the promise-remnant without being coextensive with it, so 9:32 states why the nation's flesh-defined pursuit failed rather than what distinguishes the remnant inside it. Romans 11:7 is Paul restating the same contrast with *the elect* as the term.

**That 9:16's jurisdiction is the question of 9:6.** The chapter exists to answer why Paul's kinsmen do not believe. Isaac at 9:7–9 and Jacob at 9:10–13 are the cases by which he answers it, and 9:16 is the conclusion drawn from them. An objector who restricts the verse to two patriarchs has to say what it then contributes to an argument about present unbelief.

**That Romans 9's mercy has distributed human objects.** The corporate reader who denies an individual election to be grounded must account for *whom* at 9:15 and 9:18, Pharaoh by name at 9:17, *us whom he has called* at 9:24, and the two brothers the argument concludes from.

**John 10:26's causal force.** A reader may take "because" as evidentiary — you do not believe, which shows you are not of my sheep. The causal weight is carried by 10:26 itself, where *because* stands between the not-believing and the not-belonging in the ordinary direction. Verse 27 is weaker than the earlier formulation allowed: predicating hearing of the sheep does not by itself put the hearing downstream, since a property may be constitutive, evidentiary, simultaneous, or consequent. What 10:27 supplies is that hearing and following characterize the sheep, which fits the causal reading without establishing it.

**Acts 13:48's *tetagmenoi*.** A perfect middle/passive participle, ordinarily rendered "appointed" or "destined." Some prefer a reflexive middle sense — as many as had disposed themselves. The form allows either. The passive is the more natural reading lexically and in context, and it is the one the versions take; the reflexive sense asks the ambiguous form to carry a less usual meaning in this construction.

**Romans 11:20 and 11:23.** "They were broken off because of their unbelief, but you stand only through faith." And three verses later: "if they do not persist in unbelief, will be grafted in."

This is the sharpest objection in the paper's own territory, and the means/ground distinction does not answer it by itself. Step 11 argues that faith-conditioned membership in the body whose members are predestined would make the predestining contingent on faith. Romans 11 conditions olive-tree standing on faith in so many words. If the tree is that body, Paul has marked the very criterion step 2b's ledger calls unmarked.

The answer is that the tree is not that body, and Paul distinguishes them in the same chapter.

**By its composition.** "Israel failed to obtain what it was seeking. **The elect obtained it, but the rest were hardened**." (11:7) Both groups stood in the olive tree before any branch was broken off. So the tree holds the elect and the rest together, and standing in it is not the same as being among the elect. Paul makes the division inside the tree, not at its boundary.

**By its losses.** "Provided you continue in his kindness; otherwise you also will be cut off." (11:22) A branch in the tree may be removed. The Romans 8 chain removes no one: whom he justified, he also glorified. A set that can lose members is not a set that loses none. So olive-tree membership and the set of 8:29–30 are different sets, whatever else is true of either.

Given that, the faith-condition at 11:20 is a condition on standing in the covenant people as it stands in history. Paul does not state it of the company that runs to glory without loss. That does not show the company has no faith-condition of its own, since two sets can differ and still share a condition. It shows only that 11:20 does not supply one, and so it does not supply the marker the criterion model needs.

**Matthew 22:14.** "Many are called, but few are chosen." The two words are set on opposite sides — *klētoi* wide, *eklektoi* narrow — while this paper treats calling as a term that tracks election, on 9:11–12's "his call," 9:24's "us whom he has called," and Romans 8:30's chain in which everyone called is justified. The answer is that the paper argues Paul's usage and Matthew's parable is not Paul's. In Romans 8:30 the called are coextensive with the justified and the glorified by the sentence's own construction; in Matthew the called are the invited. An objector who wants Matthew's sense in Romans has to break 8:30's chain to get it.

**Deuteronomy 30:1–2.** Before verse 6's heart-circumcision, the passage describes Israel returning to the LORD and obeying. So step 13 is a poor text from which to read off a general order of divine change before human response, and the paper does not use it for that. What verse 6 gives is that the heart-circumcision is God's act and love of God is its stated result. Step 13 is corroborative and the thesis does not rest on it.

**Deuteronomy 30:6's scope.** A reader may hold that it concerns a future restoration of Israel rather than the standing way God deals with men. The answer is that 10:16 and 30:6 name the same act, and that step 12's physical pattern is already in place.

---

## Not claimed here

How many are outside the class, or that any person other than Esau was passed over. What Esau's final state is. That anyone is hardened, or why. That no property a man does not perform bears on his election. That the faith-criterion reading is unavailable: this paper shows only that the reading with no criterion is better supported, and the synthesis completes the step. Total inability, particular redemption, perseverance.

Nor is it claimed that the man does not believe. He does, and the believing is his.

## Index of objections

Each objection is stated in one sentence at its strongest, with where the paper answers it. This is a finding aid, not a second argument; the answers are in the body, at the point where the objection arises.

**On the hinge**

*9:16 states the source of mercy, not what selects its objects.* — Step 2a. The verse is the *ara oun* of two person-cases, 9:15 and 9:18 are distributive, and 9:18 contrasts mercy with hardening.

*The implied subject of 9:16 is the fulfillment of God's purpose, not the identity of its objects.* — Step 2a, the ellipsis paragraph.

*God can be the source of mercy and still apply a criterion; naming the decider does not exclude a rule the decider uses.* — Step 2b. Granted as a logical possibility. The answer is comparative: 9:11–12, 9:14, 9:15–18, and 11:7 repeatedly put the explanatory terms on God's side, while the criterion model must insert an unstated lower-level rule; 9:19 is used only as weak corroboration because the hardening clause can motivate the objection by itself.

*The injustice objection at 9:14 arises from the pre-birth timing alone, not from the absence of a criterion.* — Step 2b, eighth plank. Available, and it costs the objector a resolvable charge left standing through 9:15–18 and answered at 9:20 by refusing the questioner's standing. It also requires supplying a hidden criterion rather than locating one, in an objection Paul himself constructs and answers. Unlike 9:19, this objection has no alternative motivation in the immediate context, since hardening is not yet in view.

*The injustice charge at 9:14 comes from covenant fidelity: how can Israel's exclusion square with God's word and Israel's privileges (9:3–8)?* — Step 2b, eighth plank. Not answered in the body. Paul raises that question at 9:6, "It is not as though the word of God had failed," and answers it through 9:13. The question at 9:14 is a different one. It asks about injustice, and it follows directly on 9:13: "I have loved Jacob, but I have hated Esau." So what 9:14 asks about is the discrimination between the twins, and Paul answers it at 9:15–18 by restating that discrimination.

*2 Thessalonians 2:13's en-phrase modifies "chose," not "salvation," so faith is grammatically attached to the choosing.* — Step 5a. The attachment is granted, and the objector does not need *because of*, since a means can be a condition. The verse alone does not decide whether belief is the means by which the choice is carried out or the means by which a man comes to be among the chosen. 1 Peter 1:2 and 2 Thessalonians 2:14 favor the first. The result is comparative and is weighed at 2b.

*Twenty verses show that a man must believe.* — Granted throughout, and the paragraph on the remaining disagreement at the end of step 2b says why they do not touch the thesis. The dispute is not whether faith is necessary but which side supplies the differentiating term where the election of this man rather than that one is explained.

*The idem per idem is intensive rather than indefinite, so 9:15 asserts God's freedom rather than the absence of a rule.* — Step 2b. The dispute is named there and decided on the Exodus 3:14 parallel and the grammatical literature, not on what the premise yields; 2b also states why the yields-argument fails.

*9:19's objection is motivated by the hardening at 9:18, so it is not evidence that the reader took 9:16 to exclude a criterion.* — Step 2b, and granted there. The verse is not load-bearing; at most it corroborates the pattern because Paul answers without introducing a human criterion.

*John 6:40 states the Father's will with believing in it, between two verses the paper cites.* — Step 14. It says who has life, not what grounds the giving at 6:37.

*John 6:64 has Jesus knowing from the first who would not believe.* — Step 14. The observed unbelief is the occasion of the saying. The saying states a necessary condition — no one can come unless granted — and the step does not infer from a man's not coming that the grant was withheld.

*James 2:5 names poverty as a property of the chosen.* — Step 7b. Poverty is non-responsive and falls outside what the thesis denies. The grammar points the same way: *rich in faith* and *heirs* are predicate accusatives defining *the poor*, so faith characterizes what the chosen are as chosen rather than a qualification they brought.

*Romans 11:32 says God will be merciful to all.* — Step 6. Named there. In context the *all* is Jews and Gentiles as groups, and it leaves the distributive *whom* of chapter 9 where it is.

*Matthew 22:14 separates called from chosen, so calling is not an election-term.* — Inference list. Paul's chain at Romans 8:30 makes the called coextensive with the justified; Matthew's parable uses the word for the invited.

*Acts 10:35 makes fearing God and doing right what makes a man acceptable.* — Step 2a, the third-candidate paragraph. *Dektos* concerns whose devotion God regards across national lines, and Cornelius has yet to hear the message by which he will be saved (11:14).

*Foreknowledge only moves when God knew, so 9:11's pre-birth clause is chronology and nothing more.* — Step 4. Paul subordinates the clause to a purpose: so that the purpose of election might stand, not by works but by his call. On a foreseen-condition model the appeal loses its force, since the foreseen act would still do the differentiating.

*Willing and running are two human activities; another human fact might still be proposed.* — Step 2a. Granted as a possibility. The paper does not classify all other human acts as works. A proposed candidate needs a text making it the condition of election, and it must be responsive to touch the thesis at all; step 2b's generic test then applies.

**On faith**

*Faith is a condition of receipt, not a source; the mercy still comes from God.* — Step 5. A condition that picks this man is a distinguishing human fact.

*Faith is not a meritorious work, so it is not what 9:16 excludes.* — Step 5, the merit reply. Granted and beside the point.

*The thesis is narrowed to responsive facts, so faith escapes it.* — Step 5. It does not. Responsive is not the same line as meritorious. Faith is the man's own act by this paper's own grant, so it is responsive and sits inside the class the thesis denies.

*Faith is receptive trust rather than willing, so Paul's two words do not lexically exclude it.* — Steps 2a, 2b, and 4. Granted. Faith is tested as the actual proposed criterion: works fall to 9:11, and the cumulative ledger at 2b asks whether faith can be inserted beneath Paul's God-side explanatory terms.

*Paul denies two named activities; the thesis denies every responsive condition, which is a wider negation.* — Step 2a. The universal is not inferred from the lexical range of two words, nor from classifying all other human acts as works. Works are excluded by name; faith is tested separately; a third candidate must be shown in a text to function as the condition of election.

*God foreknew who would believe and elected accordingly.* — Step 4a. Foreknowledge relocates when God knows the willing, not what the election rests on. The position is fork two; whether faith occupies fork two is weighed at 2b, and the synthesis completes it.

*2 Peter 1:10 tells believers to "confirm your call and election," and 1:11 ties that to entering the kingdom, so election turns on what they do.* — Not answered in the body, because it does not bear on the ground. The Greek is σπουδάσατε βεβαίαν ὑμῶν τὴν κλῆσιν καὶ ἐκλογὴν ποιεῖσθαι — be diligent to make your calling and election firm. What is confirmed is a call and election already in place. Verse 11 says what follows for those who do it: "entry into the eternal kingdom of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ will be richly provided for you." So the diligence bears on entering the kingdom, and this paper does not dispute that. What neither verse says is what God's choosing was contingent on. Confirming an election is not the same act as grounding it.

*John 3:16 makes life turn on believing.* — The willing-texts section. It states who has life, not what grounds the choosing — the same distinction the paper draws at John 6:40 and Romans 10.

*1 Corinthians 1 names weakness and lowliness, which are facts about the chosen and are tied by purpose clauses to the choosing.* — Step 7b. Weakness, foolishness, and low birth are non-responsive: no act of the man constitutes them. The thesis denies contingency on what the man does, so the objection is granted in full and lands outside what is claimed. The neuter plurals and *ta mē onta* corroborate that Paul designates a kind rather than a qualification each member satisfied, but that second answer is comparative and the scope answer is what disposes of the objection.

**On the class**

*9:16 is Paul's conclusion from two patriarchs and does not govern later believers.* — Step 11. The chapter exists to answer why Paul's kinsmen do not believe; Isaac at 9:7–9 and Jacob at 9:10–13 are how he answers it.

*Election is corporate, not individual.* — The front matter. Three models carry that name. On two of them God's choice determines the roster, and the objector has conceded the object of the thesis while disputing its ground. Only the corporate-first model — God chooses the body, faith determines who is in it — is distinctively corporate, and step 11 answers it.

*God elected a body; persons join it by faith, so there is no individual election to ground.* — Step 11 and the corporate paragraph in the front matter. Romans 9's mercy and calling have distributed personal objects, and Romans 8:29–30 carries one set of objects from an antecedent predestining to a personal justifying, with 9:24 tying chapter 9's calling to that chain.

*Romans 11:20 and 11:23 condition olive-tree standing on faith, which marks the criterion the ledger calls unmarked.* — The inference list, under Romans 11. The tree is not the set of Romans 8:29–30: 11:7 puts the elect and the hardened rest inside it together, and 11:22 allows a branch to be cut off while the Romans 8 chain loses no one. Paul states the faith-condition of the first set, not of the second, so 11:20 does not supply the marker. Whether the second has a faith-condition is weighed at 2b.

*Personal mercy and calling do not establish an antecedent personal election; election may be corporate with the other two personal.* — Step 11. Purpose and calling stand together at 8:28 and again with election at 9:11, and Romans 8:29–30 predicates the predestining of persons and runs them to justification and glory, which is the determination the front matter defines as the object under review. The corporate advocate may delimit that set as he likes; he still owes an account of what conditions a person's being in it.

*Predestining persons who are in the class to glory is not selecting persons into the class.* — Step 11 grants this. The paper does not claim selection-into-the-body. It claims an antecedent act with a person as object and salvation as terminus, and asks what conditions it.

*I simply do not call that act election.* — The front matter defines the term and says why: a label dispute is not an answer. The question is whether the determination is contingent on a responsive human qualifying condition.

*Romans 8:29 begins with foreknowledge, so citing the chain imports foreseen faith.* — Step 11 cites the chain for the locus of election only and says so; what conditions the election is taken up at 4a and 2b.

*Ephesians 1:13 has sealing follow believing.* — Front matter. It says when a man is sealed, not what grounds his being chosen.

*John 6:70 has Jesus choosing Judas, so "choose" need not mean a choosing to salvation.* — Step 8a. Granted, and cited there; it is why 8a is used for initiative only.

*Romans 11:28 calls unbelieving Israel beloved "as regards election," so election language covers people outside the Romans 8 company.* — Not answered in the body. Granted: "as regards election they are beloved, for the sake of their ancestors; for the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable" (11:28–29). The word is used there of the nation in relation to the patriarchs, and the ground it names is the ancestors, not a response of the persons in view. The paper does not claim that every occurrence of the word names the determination of Romans 8:29–30. Step 11 argues that identity from 8:28–30, 8:33, and 9:11, not from the word alone.

*Genesis 22:16–18 gives the blessing "because you have done this," so a response conditions the covenant.* — Not answered in the body. The oath comes after God chose Abram (Gen 12:1–3), made the covenant (15:18), and named Isaac (17:19). It confirms with an oath what had already been given. It does not show that the earlier choosing waited on the later act. "Election in the Abrahamic Covenant Is to Salvation" takes up the obedience clauses at its step 3.

*Nehemiah 9:8 says God found Abraham's heart faithful and made a covenant with him, so faith preceded the covenant.* — Not answered in the body. The prayer gives the order itself: God "chose Abram" (9:7), then "found his heart faithful before you," then "made with him a covenant" (9:8). The faithful heart comes after the choosing and before the covenant. The prayer does not give it as the ground of the choosing.

**On Romans 9 itself**

*9:32 names faith as the differentiator, in the same argument.* — Step 11. 9:31's Israel is the national body of 9:6's descent-referent, and 11:7 has Paul restating the same contrast with *the elect* as the term.

*9:30's Gentiles attained by faith, and that is a human fact distinguishing persons.* — Step 11. Attaining is the means; the calling is what 9:16 concerns.

*Mercy and hardening are parallel at 9:18, and Exodus puts human agency on the hardening side.* — Step 2a, the hardening paragraph.

*9:19–20 shows Paul answering an objection this paper will not answer.* — Front matter. Paul anticipates it; what 9:20–24 does with it is not taken up here.

*Jeremiah 18:7–10 has the potter change what he does with a nation when it turns, and Paul's potter at 9:20–21 draws on the same image.* — Not answered in the body. Jeremiah's referents are nations and the dealings God announces with them, to pluck up or to build, and the nation's turning conditions what God does with it in history. The election under review is the antecedent assignment of a person to salvation. Applying Jeremiah's conditional to it requires a further relation that the image does not supply. And Paul's own question at 9:21 is about the potter's right over the clay, not a condition the clay meets.

**On the illustration**

*That B does not believe today does not make B non-elect.* — The question section. The scene displays the question and rules on neither man.

*If every man eventually believes, no A/B contrast exists, so a faith-condition escapes fork two.* — Answered twice. The definition at the front and fork two: a condition does not stop being one because everyone meets it, and the contrast is how the paper tests for a condition rather than what makes something one. Then 2a's per-instance reading of 9:16, which carries 2b's result to the ground of each election without reference to whether another man fails it. Step 6a adds that the contrast case is not empty in any event, since Esau is placed outside the class by Hebrews 12:17.

*Esau lost a paternal blessing, not an eternal inheritance, so step 6a proves nothing about the class.* — Step 6a. Genesis 28:4 names the thing conferred as the blessing of Abraham, and the companion paper argues that inheritance terminates in salvation. An objector who reads ἀπεδοκιμάσθη as rejection from one occasion owes an account of why Hebrews builds a warning against falling away on a man who lost only a paternal blessing.

*Ishmael was excluded from the covenant too, so the same argument would exclude him — and Scripture says God blessed him.* — Step 6a, and granted. The argument does not run on Ishmael, because Genesis 17:20 and 21:20 render no verdict on the man. Esau is the case Scripture decides.

*Step 10 claims the new birth precedes faith.* — It does not. John 1:12 has believing before the giving of the right and 1:13 has the birth from God; the paper takes the second without relating it to the first. Step 10 is source evidence, not order evidence.

*Uncaused believing is a real possibility, so fork three stands.* — Step 2. Uncaused believing is not fork three. If God elects those who in fact believe, the brute faith is the condition and the position is fork two; if he elects on no condition, the brute faith bears on nothing. Fork three requires holding that the discriminatory mercy itself has no ground.

*God's mercy is the same for everyone, so there is no discriminatory election to ground.* — Step 6. Not a fork of the trilemma but a denial of its subject; answered from the distributive *whom* at 9:15 and 9:18 and from the cases 9:16 concludes out of.

**Not objections to this thesis**

That God produces the believing — not claimed here. That any person other than Esau was passed over, or how many are outside the class — not claimed. Esau's final state — not claimed. That a non-responsive property of the man may bear on his election — not denied here. Total inability, particular redemption, perseverance — other arguments. Why God finds fault — bracketed, and raised by Paul at 9:19.
